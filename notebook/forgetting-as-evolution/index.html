Forgetting as Evolution

1100.001 · 1100 intelligence and learning · essay · 2024.06.26

I want more people to talk about forgetting.

I want more people to think about the past and recognize that what they remember is only what was necessary, and that their lives are something that comes from the construction of a personal narrative, not from what we specifically chose to remember.

I think this is what troubles me the most - that at some point we conflated the idea of memory with the idea of permanence and because of this caught ourselves in the trap of remembering everything. Now, overloaded by the burden of this sort of rationalist thinking, we're trying to work our way back to a place that can make more sense of the world using heuristics rather than what we've been told to do - attempt to keep it all in our heads and then fault ourselves with the loss of what wasn't important.

A friend of mine sent me an article this morning about how it is that Gen Z people filter for information in the modern age, that it's no longer about the process of trying so hard to filter for truth and instead filter for what it is that their peers say about the subject1, because truth has become so maleable. It purports thats what we used to consider sources of truth are now so lacking in credibility that we've reached a point where an entire generation has essentially written off the idea of traditional journalism, making way instead for something that approximates relative truth, or what one could call 'truth by local consensus'.

Even the more current thinkers decry a state where this feels like something that should spurn concern rather than excitement - Klippenstein just wrote about it in his substack - but I find that there's something more fearful and less reasonable about taking this approach over, say, optimism.

In some ways, the trend toward truth by local consensus heartens me - it points toward a distrust of authority that feels more democratic rather than one explicitly oriented toward institutional credibility. When we step back to take it in, this says more about the lack of credibility in institutions as it stands2. There's some trepidation that can be had around the idea that local consensus can lead to disinformation as communities trend toward echo chambers that reflect back their own logic, but I have more optimism than this. I see those who participate in this form of truth-seeking as inoculating themselves against a firehose of institutionalized (mis)information - and it's this part that makes me think that maybe forgetting is something that we're supposed to do more of at the organismal level.

To me, forgetting does not mean that information is being lost and can't be retrieved - it's something more nuanced than this, instead its a process of pruning information for what is actually relevant. This is why it rhymes with the process of seeking truth, but more importantly it is how humans participate in making sense3 of the world around us. Forgetting is the process of taking what is relevant and allowing ourselves to settle into what the current of time pulls away from us - and I think there's something to embracing this as a natural process.

There are further implications to this that aren't so woo - thinking of forgetting in this way is valuable for practical application - and it's valuable to consider how this affects the landscape of logic that exists around how we create technology. Biomimicry was once (and should remain) a staple of forefront design, so there's no reason not to think about forgetting as an evolutionary process first and foremost (it literally is).

The present era is one that focuses so much on storing boundless amounts of information from even deeper wells of truth, and this is, broadly, a deviation from how information is managed in most natural systems. No one can tell me that every permutation of a tree was important to an oak, and no one can tell me that all the ways that a river can run are important to the way that one particular river flows. All information is local and adapted to circumstance, and the fidelity of that information is a product of its relevance to the environment it's a part of, not the entire body of possibility that precedes it - this would be immensely inefficient.

It's for this reason that I think forgetting is important - it's something that we need to learn how to do gracefully. Truth by local consensus is the human species adapting locally to the flow of information across the entirety of the internet. Zero-Knowledge Proofs are a mathematical repercussion of the same conclusion - a striving toward remembering only the truth that is relevant on the basis of the local information that proves it. Even the learning methodologies of the Machine Learning algorithms that support artificial intelligence depend on setting paths through knowledge4, regardless of how their datasets can be polluted.

It's not a question of doing so aimlessly - there's value in learning how to forget gracefully. Truth by local consensus is a form of adaptation, and on the landscape of an internet that by any measure of human history is still incredibly young5, humans are still figuring out how to adapt to consuming more information in a week than someone 100 years ago might have consumed in their entire lifetime. It stands to reason that we need to learn to trust the process of forgetting and to learn how to do it in a way that doesn't compromise our ability to reason.

There's also a finer point to put on this - I don't mean by any of my writing up to this point that you should throw caution to the wind and forget everything that you've learned. What I'm saying is that there's a significant cognitive burden in thinking that you have to remember it all, and this is not only unhealthy6 but is also something that you should release yourself from thinking you have to keep up with everything. We're not designed to think in terms of everything we've remembered, we're only meant to remember what is important to us based on the circumstances relevant to direct experience we have of living.

Forgetting is a process of accepting that access to boundless information does not mean maintaining access it all. More expansively, it also means treasuring that which you do remember, because it is likely that the natural course of things is to remember that which is most important to you.
