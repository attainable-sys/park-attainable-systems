You're not on Sabbatical

1050.044 · 1050 writing · essay · 2026.08.05

A few weeks ago, I attended a writer’s workshop with Olive Tree Writing Club (highly recommend if you’re in NYC and have the time). When we went around the room introducing ourselves, everyone described what they wrote about and what everyone did for work, and after three separate people said they planned to write about the ‘intersection of tech and philosophy’, it turned out that about 20% of the room claimed to be ‘taking a break’ or explicitly ‘on sabbatical from work’. I find the language being used here interesting. I can’t recall perfectly whether or not I’m right in saying this, but I’m almost certain all of them were ex (or current) tech workers. That tells me something. I think there’s really only one difference between being ‘on a sabbatical’ and being ‘unemployed’.

I certainly haven’t rested. During this imposed ‘sabbatical’ I have: applied to graduate school, trained for a marathon, run a marathon, run from one city to another through the desert, attempted to write a lengthy and ambitious work of fiction that is still in ambiguous progress, and trained for another marathon. I did not actually take any ‘time off’, and instead I have mostly treated this time as an extended performance of endurance and self-flagellation. This has culminated in my present state, where I am physically tired all the time, financially less for wear, but maybe having learned something, if anything, from the time I’ve spent riding the blinds toward whatever comes next.

What do you mean you want time off

The traditional definition of the sabbatical is an earned departure from a place of work coming from having ‘paid one’s dues’ in time and effort, with this period ending in a return to secure, normalized employment. A sabbatical is or has been most common in tenured academic environments, with Harvard being the first school to offer it in 1880. The sabbatical is meant to be a recognition of the amount of work that goes into being an intellectual and is presented as an opportunity to step away from one’s work, with some level of certitude that there is a job to come back to.

The academic standard set was that this time would be used ‘productively’, to either result in some personal body of work or simply as a way to recover from professional burnout. At some point in the last 50 or so years, the affordance was ported over to the traditional workforce, with sabbaticals usually ranging somewhere between 4-6 weeks of leave on the basis of having worked some set amount of years at a company, with some of these sabbaticals paid, partially paid, or unfunded, but always with the guarantee that there’s a job to come back to.

Security.

I said, what do you mean you want time off.

This is decidedly not the way that I have heard people in my life use the term. No one that I have known to say that they are ‘taking a break’ have been taking 4-6 weeks off as a result of working at a company for 10 years, because no one I know has worked at a company for 10 years. Those I have met who claim to be on sabbatical are doing so for up to a year, with the majority of these people saying that they are ‘figuring out what they want to do next’. It means that the people I know who are choosing to use the term are really just choosing not to work and conjuring the faith that on the other side of this period of ‘rest’ they remain employable and necessary to some work environment or other, thus allowing them to bank on this security. More power to them (and myself) for this.

It takes guts to think you’re worth that much.

The sabbatical is a structure put in place when the work environment was constructed around a relationship between a worker and the company or institution to which they belonged. A sabbatical, along with decent healthcare, childcare, a guaranteed pension or long term return, were included in the suggestion of work - you take care of your tools, your tools take care of you.

That structure has all but eroded - health care at most companies is a major expense that actively works against those who want to use it, no where provides anything for childcare save the embattled parental leave, and it seems impossible to imagine staying at a company long enough to imagine that they’d be willing to retire you, so whatever pithy pension was paid into after the good luck of a job that contributed to a 401k is all you have to live on, in the end. The only thing that matters anymore is how much you’re getting paid.

There’s wonder in not knowing what you were made for

There’s an expression in Spanish I sit with quite often. ¿A qué te dedicas? Most people would translate it directly to 'what do you do for a living,' but directly translated it means 'what are you dedicated to?'. I think at some point in the past it might have been possible to say that you are dedicated to a job that takes care of you for the work that you put into being there. Now, with that relationship gone, workers seek to remain dedicated to themselves, to find purpose outside of the false, wavering mission of a company that doesn’t make them feel valuable. Hence, those with the means take a break to figure out how to develop self-worth.

The thing is, I know a lot of people on sabbatical right now. More than one might expect, and I don’t know if it’s normal for this many people to be on sabbatical at the same time. Demographically those I have met who are claiming this privilege skew toward high-achieving people from corporate environments who found some level of status in their careers, and the general sense I get from them is that they are exhausted. I sure am. If you have some money set to the side, it feels a lot better to call unemployment a ‘sabbatical’, because that allows us to brush off the uncertainty to an unknown point in the future and (in theory) should allow us a degree of rest. Hopefully (for some) it gives us the time to also answer a question of ‘what the fuck do I actually want to do’ when the workforce lets you know it doesn’t really care whether or not you are a part of it.

The distinction is more obvious than I think we’re admitting to ourselves. Claiming that one is ‘on sabbatical’ belies an exhaustion at the prospect of re-entering the workforce and participating in the humiliation ritual that is the job search. It is the reason that among this population of those ‘at rest’ there is a pervading sense that maybe, just maybe, it is possible to remain in this state of self-directed regulation in our relationship to work.

The perfusion of the ‘portfolio career’ is exactly this ambient desire - a hope that there is a way to remain employable without ever really having to be directly employed - thus magically avoiding the insecurity, obligation, and lack of agency that comes from committing ourselves, our lives, our time, and our energy to a mechanism that works us to the bone until the day it suddenly decides that part of its body isn’t necessary anymore and lops it off, leaving all the muscles, nerves, and capability that existed in that limb twitching, amputated, unmoored and questioning its value as anything more than vestigial. Oracle fired 21,000 employees last week. I’m upset, clearly. Too many of my friends have been laid off from what they thought was their dream job.

Thus I can’t blame anyone claiming a sabbatical over their actual state - being unemployed is stressful. It confers a deep doubt that we know what we are doing or that we are even worth anything at all, and in a society that values employability above all else as a metric for measuring worth, it is difficult to feel this unmoored. If a person has the privilege of money to take a break, it is likely because they pursued a career so endlessly that they didn’t pursue anything that might have resulted in other responsibilities, such as children, mortgages, or other dependencies. I can’t imagine someone taking a modern sabbatical without money.

Sometimes the blinds swing shut and you realize you are in a cold, dark room, alone.

Those of us with the means to do so are all ‘on sabbatical’, but specifically the vertigo of the statement is ‘from work’. Implied here is that ‘work’ will simply take us back at some point.

But we’re really just unemployed.

If work is love made visible, then rest is the love you give to yourself in return for the effort of work. That is, ideally, what a sabbatical should offer.

The fine line between sabbatical and the sheer cliff of unemployment is mostly financial anxiety. I think people who have a ton of money to spare are far more likely to take their time in finding a new job, whether or not their period of unemployment is willful. That’s just sort of how it is. Congrats, if you’re there. I mean it. We worked way too hard, we don’t know how to rest, and we aren’t sure what if anything we can do next.

For my part, there was a shift when I got to the 100,000 word mark in my book, where I realized that all the work I’d put into it was not necessarily going to pay the bills. I’d taken enough of a break to constitute rest, all while rigorously sticking to a writing schedule that got all those words out in 4 months — but the book isn’t done. I had to change tactics. Which meant I had to reconsider my runway for getting it done.

I ran from LA to Las Vegas in April, and I came back feeling like everything had fallen apart - any aspiration that I had around the book lost steam and I began to wonder if it was worth working on. Up until that point, the sabbatical had a point: something to prove, more to myself than anyone else — and the moment that I doubted it, I shifted from sabbatical to unemployment. Now there’s pure uncertainty on the horizon with the desire for certainty in how I afford to keep going. That wasn’t there before when I felt like I had momentum. Any time you stop for too long, you start to wonder if you have the energy to keep going.

However what I learn from running in places like the Mojave at night always brings me back to the same knowledge; the future is a yawning abyss filled with monsters you never know if you’re going to meet, but even in that dark you have to keep putting each foot in front of the other. I’m not on sabbatical. I’m just running toward something different. Endurance requires that you keep going.

What are you dedicating that forward movement to?
