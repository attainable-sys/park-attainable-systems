Will the mountain be reforged?

1011.002 · 1011 endurance · essay · 2026.08.27

On the OCC track today, watching runners filter by. It is 10 in the morning, they have run 23km so far, and there's much more to go. They are running through Trient, a blip of a town in Switzerland, and it's hard not to feel completely arrested by the landscape around us. All the while, I'm lookin up at a glacier that appears, more each year, to be merely holding on. The change is happening too slowly for anyone to witness directly, but it's happening faster than anyone is likely willing to admit. All the valleys around Montblanc are valleys of inspiration, and I cannot help but ask myself how much longer it will remain inspiring.
It might also be the people who will keep this inspiration in motion. These are people who will continue to show up to these races even after the glacier is gone, either out of a desire to continue testing themselves, or (hopefully) out of respect for what the mountain is meant to be. Mont Blanc. The white mountain.
This week, however, a dam burst on another glacier and a wall of water careened down the same kind of scenic valley. The footage is terrifying. None of it looks real, conjured out of an imaginarium of inescapable calamity. Every video I have seen of it is filled with people who I know, instantly, are dead. That part of Nepal is ruined. Their mountain gave way, and now the valley has been scraped clean - much in the same way that glaciers tend to, but much more violently, gathering all that remains before it in one miasma.
In Trient, I'm leaning on a bench with my feet up in the sunlight as we wait for our friend to show up. It is balmy today. She is the one running OCC - a sports influencer with a heart of gold. She is the one who shows me the first video of Nepal, the night just before she wakes up to run.
I watch a woman rush up behind a runner in her cycling cleats, and shouts encouragement after him - Allez Jerôme!, and the calls are more desperate as he gets further from her, Allez Jerôme!, harboring hope and fear at once. The choice to engage in endurance is a test, yes, and particularly one filled with fear of not knowing how we will return. The desperation in the woman's voice is one we all know well - we didn't know how we'll come back any time we have tried; a little changed, very tired, well-tested against conditions.
And so if the mountain shrugs of itself its cap of snow - should the name change? Or will it be hardened, when all the water is gone and rock is all we see, will its name be a memory of what it had once been?
There is no mystery to me. Already all the great footraces of the world against which athletes bash their bones to divine their futures are measured under the forbearance of this mountain's name. The 'MB' will one day remain a contraction, and nothing more, and we will continue to run circles around the mountain, forgetting that there was a time where we couldn't, because the white of that glacier's cap came so far down that the route wasn't there to take. This is not an indictment. Why would I? It's human. Thousands just died in Nepal, and people will still go back to the mountains to make themselves feel big. I probably will too.
Respect does not necessarily come with service in hand, nor rather it is only veneration when no service comes with what we've chosen to call respect. Running through this place is not respecting it. It's worship without supplication. We do nothing for the mountains.
All the same I sense a turning, I think, A sickening guilt of not leaving anything for whomever comes next, abject fear of realizing that the bite we have taken is too large and we must yet swallow it, with the friction of its mass grating its way down our throats. Damn the future. Whose it is anyway?
I am still proud of my friends and the efforts we undertake. I think of them sometimes as a form of thanks, but in the way an ant can thank us for not crushing them below our heel. Partially I can enjoy this remove and show up to these events because I know at some point we will learn our lesson. I'm not expressing a fear that the future will turn out terribly. It already is. We don't even tally the numbers of the dead anymore.
I think when we have vomited fprth the build of having destroyed most beautiful things, we'll see that guilt transform us. I think about katharsis and its literal meanings - contrary to what the Czech writer Milan Kundera had to say about vomiting, I invite us all to purge. I think it is necessary.
This year, the trophies for the winners of UTMB received 40 trophies across races made by Andy Parkin. They are all made out of trash. The base is scorched wood from when the second floor of the 300-year-old mill he call his studio burned down the first time. the landscape over which the runners traverse in his trophy are tin cans left behind by the melting glacier, or aluminum that smelted into slag and fell into the basement, or aluminum and copper sheeting from roofs and storms across the valley that he'd gone out to find. Maybe the trash the glaciers of Montblanc pushes to the surface are a form of vomiting, and when the bite we have taken has risen, it is heaving with only blood like phytobacteria left before bone. Maybe when the snow is gone, it will finally feel clean, changed. Reforged.
When Andy Parkin fell climbing, he decided he'd keep living. He said that he came back likely stronger than he was before he fell. He is his own artwork, a continuous trying that resulted in the person I got to meet. His paintings evolved. I like his dark period, but no one in ultrarunning knows who his is, or who he was to climbing.

  • I think my issue with this essay is that it's good, thematically, but it's not focused on what actually needs telling:
    • we are running around a dying mountain like ants confused by their own pheromones
    • Andy Parkin makes art out of the glacier's trash.
  • Revolution?
    • ca ira

I cannot divorce endurance and revolution because from either we experience the same. I do not think that running is revolutionary, but I do think that what it teaches can be.

Structure:

Who do I really want to be?
I seem to want to work in ultra-endurance on the one front, which is great, but I also specifically want to tell stories. I feel as though there's not a holistic interpretation of how these races matter to people, much less in the same sense that the Mountain Races in China hold an incredible appeal to me.
I think it's also important to tell stories about endurance in general - beyond the question of athleticism.
How do I line them up?
