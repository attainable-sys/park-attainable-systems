on good images

1060.002 · 1060 photography · essay

Photography functions in a fairly obvious sense: one points a camera at a world outside of oneself and imposes perspective on it through the action of taking a photograph. Despite this, life has had its way of imposing doubt on me as a photographer, and it's part of what caused me to walk away from the practice.
However, that's not what I'm writing about today, and it's not what you're going to read about - it's just a lens through which we're going to discuss what itched at me when I was trying to leave photography behind and gave me my reasons for leaving.

Good images

For a number of years, I thought photography couldn't do this. The reason I didn't was that I assumed photography was inherently self-limiting; an image is a trapped moment in time, a small death of the present: good images bewilder, great images sink into the fabric of the soul and beg us to wonder what else happened, or what should happen next.

These days, I see a lot of the first, and while I truly love the stories of those that have found a way to make money with a camera, my sense is that images that only serve an aesthetic purpose don't really compell us. If they do, an age that focuses on optics is one that tries fervently to flaunt our individuality, forcing the image-maker to consider the image as an extension of the self rather than an attempt at observation. Neither approach is necessarily the 'correct' one, but I do think that we have lost something when we consier images purely as a matter of perpective. Art is meant to ecoke a sense of awe, I think. Are we meant to believe that all matters of awe are purely at the hand of their creator? What happened to the conduit?

The Conduit

I want to widen our perspective here a little bit: when I refer to this later phenomenon, I'm speaking to an artistic moment that I believe to be the motivating force between the work of many artists: it is as though by the mere act of creating, the artist is speaking to something larger than themselves. We have a variety of names for the impetus that comes over us in the act of creation that compells us forward
I have tapped the pressed-foam ceiling tiles of this feeling a few times in my life, and I have never known exactly what to do with it. I backed away from it for a period of years, for instance, due largely to the fact that despite the fact that I knew I was on to something, I didn't really know what to do with what I was 'on to'. It's as though in the moment one realizes that what they are creating might actually hold greater significant beyond the self, it imposes an existential dread or a great reckoning: what is this 'source' from which we are drawing in our most creative moments, and what does it mean for us to have been the person who dredged it up?
The feeling is electric: it's so striking as to feel one has struck diamonds or the elixir of life itself.
The unfortunate conclusion to which some arrive here is, I think, the source of my complaint. I don't like it when an artist's response to having created something significant and memorable is to immediately think 'well then, I found a way to make something beuatiful, therefore I am the source of this beautiful thing'. Don't get me wrong, I do believe that every artist deserves credit for the work they do to perfect their craft, but we as artists are not the source of the art we create. I say this with impunity: artists are mere custodians for what life inspires within us.

Inspiration and Derivation

The core argument I'm trying to make is this: I think great things take time, but they also take commitment to tamping down the hubris of believing that we are the reason they are happening.
Inspiration requires that we pay attention to the world, either as a participant or as an observer, and I think that the greatest work we see surrounding us comes from having sit still long enough to take in the greater wonders of life and to have made something of them.

The Caption, and the lack thereof

As with every self-doubting photographer, mulling over the importance of the image brings Sontag's quote from Regarding the Pain of Others to mind:

" To the militant, identity is everything. And all photographs wait to be explained or falsified by their captions. During the fighting between Serbs and Croats at the beginning of the recent Balkan wars, the same photographs of children killed in the shelling of a village were passed around at both Serb and Croat propaganda briefings. Alter the caption, and the children’s deaths could be used and reused."
