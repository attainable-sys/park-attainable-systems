Sleeping near the VLA

1000.001 · 1000 living and loving · field note · 2015.06.11

"Slept near the VLA last night. That must be why this place feels so otherworldly. Our dogs are having the best time, the backdrop of a stellar observatory, and the easy crackling of a morning fire.
There are not many better things than this, and things that are, I've not yet experienced. Self-reliance gives us a sense of balance, and of unity where all else seems to fail providence. The emotion of this landscape and of these mornings, such as they are, this is the true essence of love. This is the freedom granted by finding oneself detached from the rest of humanity, where the world of the individual meets the whole damned universe.
This is the place where one can feel important, where conclusions can be drawn with a proper understanding of their scale and implication. There's not much to it, only separating oneself adequately to find that sense of self-importance, but as well to recognize one's connection to the grand machine that surrounds us."
(written at the Very Large Array, 2015)-- 150611 · Los Cielos Desaparecen (the sky disappears)

Having made the decision, again and again, it did not take purchase, nor precedence in his thoughts. Every morning in the courtyard he had sat for hours drinking coffee, still early before work, so early that it was as though the morning found him awake, visiting him in surprise. He was not sleeping, even returning from work he would sit in the courtyard late into the night, deactivated from motion, repeating it as though automated. The house seemed, to him, to take hold of his wrists every time he re-entered it, forcing him into its grey depths to sit in one of the three places he occupied, the stool, the plastic chair, the corner of the table. He was dimly aware of his agency, but it was the immobilizing grip of the house which forced him into silence and inaction.
On some random day, he began to avoid going home, instead wandering Oaxaca until finally remembering the existence of the bar he and Romero had visited, entering, and integrating this into a new routine, as though the poles of a magnet had suddenly reversed, and he found himself pushed away from the home, avoiding it until the bartender would nod at him to leave, and he would, and would again sit in the dark that held him just at the edge of the courtyard.
It became such that he developed a new routine of this loneliness, walking to work in the morning after hours of staring at yellow stucco that shimmered a blue in the dark, then greeting his coworkers, sorting the mail, and beginning the routine of his deliveries. The office would rotate the routes of the mailmen on a monthly schedule, meaning that at the beginning of the month he would serve one district, then later in the month cycle through the 30 or so others that made up the routes the postworkers served on foot, on bicycle, and from the office itself.
His days were, generally. unencumbered, but the conversation with the women and men who responded to his whistle and raps caused him the only emotion he felt throughout his day - he often would pause before the doors of homes, taking a deep breat order to perform the necessary gregariousness of his work.
Doña Rapaporte - her home in Xochimilco, bright pink siding, always one for more conversation that he was ready to delier. Her wrinkled frame, where hidalgo would pause within himself to head off the wave of guild and sadness suddenly overwheming his chest, for Doña Rapaporte was a widow, he was a widower, and he did not want that in common. Often he had found himself nearly wanting to shout at the woman, not to perform the sympathy or cameraderie that served only to remind him of his wife and her absence. He thought, at times, of not delivering her mail, of letting the next catero deliver the letters she would take with a thirst that disgusted Hidalgo on every face that did the same, an expression on peoples faces that invited to each person who expected money through letters the same anguished readiness to stave off destitution. The house behind her each time he arrived was grey, unmoving, the Clock radio playing, to his annoyance, perpetually throughout the entrance, so that when he engaged in the obligation of their conversations, the stilted simile of her desire to be less alone and have something in common with anyone at all would be pounctuated by the ticking, hushed tones of those onthe radio announcing the hour over the tock of each minute and second.
But he delivered the amil and contiued to be another functioning gear in the infinite clock, rotating through the town in a radial motion over the months, walking north, then east, then south, then west, repeating.
At the Palacio Federal in the mornings where they sorted the mail in the mornings, there as well would be the loose-jawed yabberings of his coworkers discussing the same things that would prime him for what Doña Rapaporte would inevitably offer as a subject of discussion, and the opinions and gossip of the other carteros would echo back to him the assents that he could offer whenever anything of substance was worth commenting on at all.
Did you hear about the operations underway in Nicaragua?
Yes, yes, so awful, that Somoza government is dangerous to its people!
Yes, but the Sandinistas are also terrible.
De que hablas, they are revolutionaries. Our own government supports them!
No pues - they are communists, I heard it myself - I have never heard of something so stupid. I have also heard that they are homosexuals, and worse that they are atheists. Where do you get your morals from if not from God?
No mames, güey. That's not true. I heard they are practicing religious revolution - Teología de Liberaçión, I heard.
That sounds like something made up.
Someone would shrug, either Hidalgo, or Doña Rapaporte, or any other old woman, or any of the carteros repeating the same lines.
At least here, the mail still moves
Yes, I did hear it is difficult to get any word out down there
Also in Honduras. And Guatemala
Oh my god
Yes, oh my God, with the earthquake
Yes, terrible
But we cannot go around fixing everyone's problems
You'r right, but we are neighbors as well, I am glad we sent money to Guatemala
Yes, but how much will it continue to cost if we must continue on helping others like this
and so on, and Hidalgo would half-listen. It seemed the world was a fiction outside of Oaxaca and he was tasked with twining the yarn through the punctuated breaths of its plot. Outside of Oaxaca was a place of which people would all tell their separate stories, each fiction fitting neatly into the foundations of the next, and into this unknowing he had cast his feelings about the location of his daughter. The earthquake in Guatemala had shaken everyone, seemingly, but him, and he had continued to stand as though a flexible pillar that had allowed the ground beneath it to shift without moving. He did not, even, feel the motivation to fear. The fear he felt when he thought of Filomena felt already like a mistake, and when he transitioned from his staring into the dark middle distance of his empty home into the dingy middle distance of his own reflection behind the bar, he expected nothing. he expected no letters, and none came beyond the first sdome five months before, from Quetzaltenango, well before the earthquake, on which he had read his daughters signature, then folded the letter back into its envelope without reading it.
It was just as well, given the distance, for him. As though Filomena herself had disappeared when the earth had opened up, and in his walks around the city., he imagined, furiously, that she must exist in some liminal space between life and death, between the politics and possibility of her survival, and for this reason, he did not wonder if she was safe. He did not read the letter, because he did not need to hear from her. He knew, already, what she must be doing. It may have been this which made him believe, distantly, that the earthquake had simply happened, but that nothing else had been affected. The fiction of the event and the adjustment to this story to which his town and those who lived within it reacted felt like a performance. It did not move him that the epicenter had been close to their neighboring country's capital, nor that it had stirred revolt, because his daughter was probably fine, and wasn't there anyway, so no one else or their sorrow really mattered. She was fine, and maybe, unfortunately, he did not care.
The conversations of his coworkers, their opinions of those to whome he had carried the mail, and the sirring weight of his emply home acted as a self-sustaining momentum, and with each swing of the pendulum, the hours would pass, vanishing under step as he continued to circle the mass at the center of his city.
The morning the adjuster showed up at his house, then, he found himself baffled at the presence of the man. The adjuster was wearing a thin, well-made suit, light for the weather, which made the man look like he was made of thin branches over which someone had pulled the fabric. Hidalgo imagined what would happen if he had picked up the man, snapping him over his knee - would it sound like the dry crack of a twig soon fed to the fire?
He did not hear the man introduce himself, and did not respond when the man said he had visited several times to meet him, but he never seemed home. The mystery of the man's presence weighed like a twined stone in the air between them until finally its thread might have snapped, and with it, the collapse of Hidalgo's somnambulent attention.
What did you say?
Si - Señor Amadeo, about the hourse, it is why I am here. I was sent by the Doña Beatriz Amelia Hidalgo de Valdivieso,-- your sister, I believe--, with the intent that I assist you with the sale of this house.
The door, which Hidalgo had been holding back with the palm of his right hand, becan to swing shut as Hidalgo released it, thinking better of the act and catching it before it swung directly into the face of the paper doll he realized was, in fact, speaking to him.
Oh,
and Hidalgo took a step back, making way for the fragile stranger to enter.
The adjustor pushed past him down the hallway, toward the courtyard, where he stood, looking up into the sky. Hidalgo was suddenly aware of a deep discomfort, a violation, which sat in his chest like a staid, spreading a blackness that seemed to grow over his vision. As the man stepped forward, furthering himself into the space that Hidalgo had kept private, Hidalgo felt himself distantly troubled by a sudden motivation to grab the nearest hefty object and smack the man over the head.
The adjustor, of course, did not notice the tension in Hidalgo's motion, and as Hidalgo reigned in the aberration of his surprise at himself, the man sat at the corner of the table in the courtyard, dusting the seat before sitting, causing Hidalgo to become suddenly aware of the thin layer of dust that seemed to cover everything in the house but the places he sat, alone, when there had been no one to visit. It was as thought the instant a stranger had entered his home, he was suddenly able to see the pallor that surrounded him, and the life that had left the space he felt fearful to follow in its departure.
The man's voice murmured as he looked toward Hidalgo with an affable smile over lips that looked like a beak, his glasses perched impossibly on the narrow divide of a nose that did not belong on so slight a human. His eyebrows raised through the syllables that Hidalgo did not hear.
Sir? Sir?
I don't think today is a good day.
Oh, I am sorry then to have bothered you, but it will only take a moment for us to discuss!
I don't want to discuss it today. I have an engagement?
the man looked back at him from the stool beside the table, his suitcase on the tarp Hidalgo had never removed since the rains.
An engagement?
Yes, Hidalgo found himself lying. I have to go
Ah okay Senor, I am very sorry to have caught you unawares.
Not a problem, please, let me see you out,
In a single motion, he picked up the man's leather folio, handing it to him, then as the man stood, placed his hand on the adjustor's back. Through the fabric, he felt the sharpness of the man's shoulderblades, and pushed him toward the gate of the house, opening the door and gently encouraging the man back into the street. On the tiled concrete just beyond the door, the man turned toward Hidalgo, and Hidalgo felt the involuntary urge to spit into the birdlike expression of the adjustor's face, whose hands were held at his sides, clutching the briefcase to his chest.
Excuse me, sir - but when do you think would be a better time?
I don't know. Not today. Some other day. I have to go.
But sir, do you want even to sell the house?
What?
The man's hawk-yellow eyes looked back at him, their whites surrounding the irises in a measured stare.
I beg your pardon, but you do indeed want to sell the house, correct? Or not?
The impulse to take the man by his lapels and smack him across the face jolted Hidalgo's hand into an involuntary twitch, and he became aware that he was sweating.
Yes.
I beg your pardon - you mean you do want to sell it?
Hidalgo strained with the effort to repeat the syllable without also letting loose a thin string of spittle into the man's face.
Brilliant! the man's eyes crinkled at their corners, and he extended a hand toward Hidalgo, not seeming to notice the tension that simmered the air between them and pulsed through Hidalgo's ears and his chest. They shook hands, and the mad produced a card, which he placed into Hidalgo's clammy palm.
When you are ready to do so, please come visit me at this address. We can start the process there. This way I do not need to come find you, and you can find me.*
Hidalgo's breath returned to his chest with a tense velocity he had to subdue into a cough as the man turned abruptly, walking down the street. Hidalgo's eyes followed him as he reached the distant corner, turned once more, and vanished from sight.
