Psychospiritual death - Patricia

1115.002 · 1115 society and its structure · essay · 2026.02.02

There is no unifying moment across all of this writing from the last week, from what I can tell. Some of it was in thinking about the artistry of words (the sidelong education found from the manner in which Powers' wrote "Playground") to the more hopeful notions of how we reshape our control of the planet. George Carlin once said that "The world doesn't care about you. Save yourselves!" implying that in the end, the world will not care about us when we are gone. Powers seems to have said the same thing. Arco, the movie I saw with Rachael this last Friday articulated the same post-disaster perspective: humans will eventually build islands in the sky to escape the destroyed surface of the planet in order to 'let the Earth rest'. The latter of these gives in to disaster - or upon reflectiion, they both do, if we're honest with ourselves.
I think there is much to be done as a result.
Today, even, I saw a news artcile showing that degradation claims on the effectivelness of solar cells over time have officially been debunked; solar cells appear to remain 80% efficient after several years of use, and the magnanimity of this result should be revelatory. In theory, it means that we can trust panels to proceed in their efficacy for longer term use than any nay-sayer might want to claim.
These are the sorts of results from within a dismal present I try to pay attention to, while still consuming my fair share of the dismal. We should celebrate victories.
However, it turns out that the interesting thing about victories is that most of them are really just a fight toward normalcy, so when we are victorious, it doesn't feel like much has happened. I've written about this before (not here, in another notebook maybe no one will ever read), but writers like Solnit are those we should probably be listening to if we're really to think about how we advance through the dismal present while still holding steadfast onto the flaming torch of Hope. I've been examining her work closely in this time because it is easy to come to the conclusion that there is no point in trying to fight. It's drawn, to me, the conclusion that maybe we should be listening to people who have already done the work. Solnit offers exactly this conclusion: we tend toward fighting for things to be normal. The issue constitutes of asking what it even means to express this normalct across teh scope of human (or even natural) experience. It is difficult to distill the notion of "normal" into relateable terms that don't leave someone else's definition out in contrast -- but I'd make the claim that maybe when we contine on the endless march toward panyt parallel freedoms, we are really trying to bring things into stability.
I think what I'm arguing here is that in teh scope of long marchest totward freedom, it isn't a question to any one victory or set of victories that the outcome might be normalcy. By another word: peace. All stories we tell charge toward the vision and desire of a society, one seeking out this final point of 'greatness' that is really just the chance to live a normal life.
Unfortunately some permutations of this 'great society' (often those coming from those already in power) do think in such zero-sum terms. For some, 'great societies' must always come at the cost of others freedom, rather than the fortune of all within tolerant outcomes. Such thinking is reductive, of course-- and doesn't consider the modalities of a world that treats resources as abundant under the same regime as intellectual abundance, at best becoming narratives of conquest-as-path-to-freedom that only think in terms of scarcity. This worldview emerges from a place of fear, and is ultimately a short-term plan. We might be able to harness the power of the sun. WQe might be able to avert the worst - given the time, intention, and effort of telling the right story.
Thinking only in terms of local victory (said local freedom) is myopic. We are beyond the myopia of regionalism as an exclusive perspective because it is too late to create a world of isolatio; we bulldozed through that possibility when we invented the steam engine, then the telephone, then the electrical grid, then the internet. The world's trash washes ashore the most remote island beaches. I'm not sure we can say the same about AI yet - but I do think that when we come to the point that all knowledge is available to everyone on the basis of nothing more than a bare-bones device that hosts all of human knowledge, there's no going back to locality. The only version that exists is bucolic; it doesn't exist except for in the imagination, or in the world that reflects values that acknowledge the uncommon scale at which any one person can truly affect the lives of any one other person across a connected planet. I'm casting stones after the latter.
In the typical syntopic sense of any good reader, the books that I've been carving through with intention reflect a parallel conclusion: what really matters is the story we are telling ourselves in response to our conditions.Solnit says this from the position of a participant, someone who has themselves shown up in situations requiring her help. Powers does this from a position of theory, but his writing seems to inspire attention to the outer reaches, where the effects of human hubris seem to make themselves felt. Powers, however, also reflects back what George Carlin says: if you want to save it all, save it for yourself. The world won't care whether or not you do, it will keep dancing whatever game it's got in mind a good while yet. It means that the reading I did in the last week oriented toward a common outward-facing conclusion: most of the mistake we make or the harm we intent to commit, are largely the result of the story we are telling, either to ourselves or to others.
To get specific, 'Playground' has Rafi - a caricature of blackness explained away by being an AI fabrications of his best friend's memories (maybe for good reason, we'll see) lives in competition with his surroundings. This could go one of two ways if we draw it out to its conclusion: either he wins and feels self-satisfied, or he loses and redoubles his efforts to win the next game. This is the dismal conclusion - but there is a third way he can play: continuously, playing forever, never actually reaching a conclusion, so that he can avoid ever having to stop playing. His life is framed through the escape from conditions, and the constant pursuit of this escape is blatantly sisyphean. Maybe he is happy, but to do so at the cost of achieving normalcy and surviving into whatever calmer version of existing victory could provide could cost all the energy it requires to do anything else. If Sysiphus is indeed happy, is he ever hungry? Do we conveniently leave this out of our narratives for freedom? Maybe he would have been happy working for the CTA, but it killed his mother, the same way it might have killed him, tablet in hand, reviewing for the nth time his own thoughts; the cause of deal that kills most black men: a heart attack, for both of them. Beating, again and again, frightened of life, until the stress of that repetition exhausts them both.
It may well be that the outcome I have drawn is that the stories we tell need their conclusions. I have not read many books that conclude otherwise: Hernan Diaz's "In the Distance" comes to ming -- but the stories we tell still need their victories to bring about at rest the ability to punctuate the local with the need to survive, given stress. Tolkien thought this was the reason that there was no point in writing more about Middle Earth - peace breeds eventual unrest in the hearts of men, and they'll find something new to fight about. The charge of the unknown is littered like shorelines with the patient labor of exploring it. It is both a fog and an interminable dark. On the way, we do discover beautiful things, and capricious conclusions might come from that beauty. These things were there whether or not we had found them, and they are not ours to keep, even when we act like they are.
Solnit's answer is that the rewriting of history is an act of reconciliation. We can change the story we tell in order to better respect those it affects, having concluded, but as well to honor these affected and those who took action against or toward those effects. Here, I think Powers has little to say. I heard he did a press tour for the book that oriented toward saving the oceans. I'm not sure why: he seemed in Playground to think this is a fantasy some AI will tell us to placate the end, a reality we can escape by telling ourselves an alternate history. This is maybe nothing more than escapism - but I think this is typical of Powers: skilled as he may be at coming up with a story, his most notable works offer a concerning conclusion that maybe the only path forward is annihilation and slow remove. So does Arco. Solnit looks as the rubble and says instead 'how can I help?'
It is ironic that this is the ultimate conclusion of which Powers convinces us, because we could hope some of his character believe otherwise. Evelyn doesn't, despite her love of the sea - she spends most of her career in teh selfish pursuit of observation and cataloguing. Nowhere in the book does Powers descrive what sahe is doing as 'activism' or 'advocacy'. Maybe Powers does think the world will keep spinning without us - but I think it is short sighted in nearly an ignorant and dangerous sense to sculpt a reader's attention toward a subject that matters and conclude that 'well, it's probably all going to die anyway, allowing all of his characters to continue on in the same vein of vague individual humanistic pursuit.
Solnit doesn't do this because she's actually seen what people can do. From what I can tell Powers hasn't done much but stand by, observe, drain out a conclusion, then cry about its beauty through beautiful prose, never having taken his own conclusion to heart as a course of action. It is the stories we tell which help us understand how to do better. Beautiful as Playground is, -- as a work of literature, that is --, it is not an act of advocacy. It's an observation, maybe made a little too close to the subject matter to be respectful of it.
I should be clear, and I do so because I mean so genuinely: I am not saying that Powers is a poor writer - but at the heart of the writing, he makes the same mistake most men do: they see the problem, grow conscious of the cause (usually them), then make little headway on actually preparing a solution.
I think he knows this, too -- he is self aware enough to articulate the perspectives of minorities despite his whiteness and gender, and he is genuinely skilled in guiding the reader to some conclusion or other: that being different from the people for whom society is built is difficult, and escaping that difficulty is harder than not having to live with it. Than being prototypically 'normal', that is. Those observations written into character hardly go anywhere, however: Evie realizes she's a lesbian. So what? Her husband dies, her children no longer exist to her, and nothing happens. Rafi dies of blackness, maybe. So what? There is not redemptive quality to this happening, it's merely a sad outcome. The residents of Makatea are smarter than globalistic noble savage perspectives of ther capacity to overcome conditions. So what? We don't know what happens next when Ina, who isn't even from Makatea (an emphasis Powers himself hamfists into a commentary on being third culture) gets all the money that Todd Keane leaves her.
This conclusion, far more than the 'gotcha' nature of Playground's conclusive arc, is what most frustrated me in hindsight. It shares the same disappointment I felt in reading the Overstory: after absolutely living in these books rich with detail, characters, settings, and thought-provoking risk - none of these provoked thoughts really concluded in anything. They don't go anywhere. I didn't learn anything. (well, that's not true, I did learn about oceanography's relatively recent inclusion into the scientific record) If literature is meant to convince us not only of the relateability of the human experience but as well to provoke new ideas, Playground only does one of those things. I was deeply affected by the most human aspects of his writing,and for this I want to extend Powers immense credit as a scholar of letters -- but to couch this relateability in the landuage and context of needful chance without drawing out a conclusion or even a lesson in this other, more pressing dimension is careless and again, specifically dangerous. It as a result becomes nothing more than thoughtful observation and unfortunately (yes, I'm going to say it) exactly as flat and predictable as what any other self-proclaimed conscious white man might have come to as a conclusion. It is advocacy without action.
I'm not surprised to realize this, bathing in the afterglow of what I truly would consider a work of art. I've been steadily drawin my own conclusion that even the most compassionate of those who observe the world from a position of (granted, unelected) power don't know what future exists beyond willfully ceding it. They lack the imagination because it does not progress the only story they have ever been told, or that they tell themselves. I also want to express my own compassion for coming to this conclusion: it is terrifying, bewildering, and altogether stymying to realize that maybe you were actually the baddie this whole time. That's as far as most of the culpable can g. It requires a staggering amount of workl to reconcile; and people in power are used to having other people do that work for them. Playground is destines to bear that same tragic subtitle of many period works; it is a product of its time: a time in which men are realizing that they caused all the failure that is steadily being brought to shore by the tide, but still haven't quite come up with what they should do in response.
So they do what all the hopeless are wont to doing: nothing at all, making art out of scraps, when maybe what they should have done was keep listening.
