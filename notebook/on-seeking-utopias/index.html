On Seeking Utopias

1055.001 · 1055 critique · essay · 2023.12.09

There's something to be said for the pull away from cynicism we’re beginning to see from the enabling effects of the new internet.

In some sense, the dystopian leanings of our predictions for humanity are starting to feel too prescient - we are living within these predictions rather than seeing them as some far-flung reality, and the experience is exhausting. It is a grueling existence made somehow more disheartening by the fact that the irrationality of humans rears its head again and again in face of what most would consider to be rational action.

So what can we do with the knowledge that we knew we would be living in the reality we are now experiencing, and that yes, indeed, it is our fault, and that yes, we also know what causes our course toward dystopia in the first place? All this in mind - extrapolations of our present into future, all the more far-flung and depressing as dystopian, must offer us pause. Do we want things to continue on course toward the fictional realities that we didn't expect to materialize? Do we let them? How much of it is under our control?

The last question I find the most urgent - if we know that present conditions are likely to keep us careening in the direction of disaster, and that we are here at our own hand - how much control are we willing to give up in the course toward a collective future, and what hand does any individual person have in ownership over control of this future?

Much of dystopian writing has us completely lacking in control - often because the faults that create a cause for dystopias have already happened, or the protagonists have little to no control over when or if they do. Any dystopia is written from an absence of control. Control is the protagonist in overcoming the conditions of being lacking thereof - admission to higher, more insidious or chaotically evil forces determine the setting. Hypercapitalist dystopias lead us into futures that elide control from individuals into a total obedience of market pressures and consumption, encouraging people to mean nothing but vectors for financial lifeblood. This is the extension of the present - capitalism alone in control, with individuals under its servitude - a financial market for every engagement and action in the material (and digital) world.

Disaster dystopias are not so different - where the bomb has already fallen, the plague has already destroyed society, the supervolcano has blotted out the sun. Here, hope can only take form in society, navigating the ashes, carving out control against the uncontrollable reality of disaster - and the solutions, paradises, or arcs for a story of exit all exist within the bounds of what is possible with disaster in control of the present. To find optimism against the face of disaster is to inspire hope in action - top take control of what little over which we can exert pressure. Optimism is a weapon against these stark realities, it is the only weapon through which to fight great improbabilities - the Hakagure's samurai facing off against certain death with no regard for their own life.

At the root of these inspired realities is a common theme - that we are not in control of the futures toward which we guided. Either we are led there by a blind and collective fault to which no individual holds blame, or there is a great external pressure over which our fates are stretched.

The most hopeful and the least human of these stories are seen as youthful fantasy - it's a hallmark of young adult fiction for a party of individuals or an individual to have sufficient control over the future that they might avoid, prove, or solve great calamity and thus save the world. That's why it's entertaining in the first place - it seems so improbable that any one group of people could both act enough in the best interest of humanity and have enough control to change the fate of the realities that we experience. It feels naïve, and it is. No one person could truly hold sufficient power to change the future, because no one person could ever hold so much control.

I think the greatest fear of humanity is to be in control and fail. It's a recurring theme in all aspects of improvement on societal and individual levels - that the hardest part of existing is giving up a desire for control when we don't have it. We hinge all aspiration on the illusion of control, and we learn to overcome frustration when we realize that we do not have control.

An Eightfold Path walks toward absolution along a tightrope of personal discipline (control) and willingness to allow existence to proceed without your influence (control). The opiate quality of a higher power, too, is thematic because the greatest situational grace for an individual is to relinquish control of their life to the determinism of a deity - to seek out calm in the recognition of a deterministic designer who can guide the present toward their intended future. Again - control of fate being handed off from the individual (or indeed the collective, with respect to the congregation) to something outside of the individual - because this makes a future that we could be in control of that much more palatable when it isn't in our favor, and puts us less at fault.

Optimism seems to sprout and grow when we take ownership over our direction - when an individual future is something toward which one person can steer, because the rudder is in their hand. There is hope in being called to action - there optimism in appreciating control as an enabling force. When we believe that we can guide fate, much more is possible. Problems feel smaller, more attainable, futures much more a vehicle for our hopes to arrive in, utopias all the more achievable.

When, then, does optimism have to do with sovereignty over control, and how does this apply to collectives, not individuals? Even blind optimism is giving oneself control over willingness to see things proceed in the most favorable direction, though not by our hand, instead by fate. But I am arguing for a more urgent sense of optimism, one that places control back in the hands of collectives, so that individuals feel that they can indeed have influence and guide collectives toward utopias.

The reason utopias feel naïve is due to an inspired sense that we are not in control - that we don't hold the keys to the future. What, then, if we allowed ourselves to believe that we did?

Dystopias do not exist when collectives are in control of the story and the futures being acted upon are in the interest of collectives. Agency is the greatest power we hold as individuals - the liberty to move freely and make choices. Dystopias sprout and fester around a lack of agency - we as individuals cannot move the path. The arc of success against dystopia is the defeat of the forces that impose dystopia and therefore maintain control. Liberty is gaining, once again, the control that you rightly deserve as an individual. Collective utopias are born from collaboration in the hands of independent agents. Cynicism against being a utopian is born from a cynicism over whether or not you have control.

How do we conceive of utopias as something attainable, when so many are under the impression that we do not have control? If it's emblematic of the present era that the futures in which we are trained are outside of our control, what better way to cope with this reality than to view hope with cynicism, and continue to believe that we indeed do not have control?

This is why systems that enable the greatest individual sovereignty precourse utopias. As it stands, we no longer feel in control of the future, and instead hope that some displaced outside hero will resolve over our present to lead us from dystopia in a hero’s journey. The truth is far more urgent, and far more in the hands of those who read this.

The power of one's own agency roots itself in immovable earth against a lack of control - the willingness to remain immobile against the flows that surround us is a protest against cynicism and giving up hope. If this is true, the agency of hopeful collectives enable even greater control over collective fate. We can then measure attainment of the much grander, more expansive utopias that agency-based optimism offers us.

We deserve utopias, but we specifically deserve several. We deserve a hopeful version of the future that is under our control, where we harness collective agency to achieve what seems under our present cynicism to be impossible. It is only impossible if we believe that reaching utopias is outside of our control.

The greatest problems of our age are borne out of giving up control to others, and some of our greatest achievements have been in favor of this control being defeated and sovereignty - liberty - returned to individuals and collectives. Climate change is a coordination problem, but it is also a problem of control - we do not believe that it is something on which we can exert control. We believe that there are other forces at play that determine the outcome. If this is true - indeed we are dystopia bound, but I do not believe that the human capacity for agency can be so easily defeated. Instead, it must be nurtured.

An agency for collectives to build away from dystopias and toward utopias requires that they, too, have agency, both in the futures that they experience and the missions that they take on with others. Technologies that enable agency are therefore imperative - because the tools of agency are what inspire an individual with the truth that they do indeed have control. Tools that we do not need to trust to control us, even more so - because tools in whose operation we have no doubt of subterfuge over our agency cannot take control from us.

We do not possess many tools that do not manipulate us in turn - we have not made many of them with intention. In the progress of human society, we must see carefully what great agency can be offered to individuals by our tools as well as the potential in these tools for robbing control that may lie in wait from their use. Oftentimes we can't predict these faults, but ownership over our mistakes is equally pressing - if we are inspired (as collectives and as individuals) to believe that we have a hand in the mistakes of humanity, we can atone for them, even learn and grow from them. To believe that there is no ownership over collective fault is dire at best, so it is imperative that we are inspired with a culture of ownership that documents and hardens ownership over our faults. If we maintain that we do not have a hand in our own mistakes, we can never seek to improve from them, and then all arguments to do so seem baseless.

It may be that the greatest human achievement is to seek means through which individuals feel collective utopias are attainable - and that these utopias are born from human intention, from the hands of individuals and collectives, and in the interest of all those that utopias must serve. Great hope unfolds from the belief that potential is an achievement at the hand of collectives, and utopias can only be achieved if great hope is afforded as a function of working toward utopias; people must be enabled to believe that utopias are possible because they themselves are capable of creating them.
