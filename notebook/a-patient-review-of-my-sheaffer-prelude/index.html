a patient review of my Sheaffer Prelude

1050.024 · 1050 writing · essay · 2026.05.30

I don't really remember what it was that encouraged me to buy the pen I'm about to write about, nor do I really understand why it is that I feel the need to review it. It's more that I feel compelled to write about it, and that I feel compelled to run against the grain of what I think has become to norm for how it is that people review things - my intent here is not to sell you on the pen I own, nor is it to necessarily critique it. It's just that I really, really love this pen, and some part of me feels compelled to write about how much I love this pen.
I have been someone who writes by hand for as long as I can remember. In fact, it's almost a little strange that I've chosen the format in which I want to write this review, as I'm writing it on my laptop, which is steadily running out of battery, and this is usually the exact reason I love a good pen - short of running out of ink, it can't die on you, so you can always write.
Now, the reasons for writing this: I was sitting on the porch at my parent's home in Virginia, reading 'Biography of X' when I looked down at the pencil I had in my hands, a Tombow Homograph Mono, and realized that I really enjoyed the pencil for the build quality in the wood. I then remembered that I, personally, was actually influenced into buying this pencil by some random video or other to buy this pencil, and I am glad that I was. It's a good pencil!
However, the reason I think it's a good pencil is not because a reviewer told me that it was a good pencil - it's the fact that I've had a box of these pencils for about two months now, and I haven't moved on from the first one I took out of the box and sharpened. In fact, I'd have to figure that I will very likely have that (beautiful) box of pencils I purchased for $14 from the internet hate machine for several more years, because, get this - I haven't even sharpened it again.
Anyway - on to more important things: what I found myself wanting to write about is that all too often, the culture of a 'review' typical of those seen across the internet exhibits two qualities:
1. It is there to sell you something
2. Most of the time, a 'reviewer' has only been using the thing they are selling you for a couple of months.
What this means, --for me-- is that while I could write paragraphs and paragraphs about the Tombow (I already have, lamentably), I actually haven't been using it long enough for me to feel honest about how it makes me feel.
I do, however, feel that I have been using my Prelude for long enough to sing its praises, but I don't really care whether or not you get one of your own. I don't think that's the point of this particular review, rather I'm trying to influence you to go out and care about something for years and years, so much so that you are compelled to come back and tell someone else just why it is that you care about that thing so much. If nothing else, that's what I feel is missing in the 'culture' of review.
Nowadays, it's not apparent that anyone claiming to 'review' something really cares for the things they own. Or rather, maybe it's apparent that some reviewers really like what they are reviewing, but not that they like any one of the actual items that they are reviewing. It is why I find it so much more delightful when I do strike upon a video or an essay where it is apparent the reviewer really does care about the thing they are talking about, because it becomes incredibly obvious through the pronounced excitement they have for what it is that they are reviewing; it appears less that they are selling one product or another, and more that they just really, really enjoy something and want to share it with the world - which is exactly why I want to write about this pen.
The reason I love my Prelude is in part because it writes well, but there is more to it than this - so much more that I don't want to say all that much about how it writes, so I'll just do that in the following paragraph. If that's all you came to read, great, we're already succeeding, and you won't need to go any further. If not, there are (I just counted) 25 more paragraphs beyond the following that explain why it is that how the pen writes is not the point:
The Sheaffer Prelude I bought in 2020 writes very well. It is perfectly balanced when posted, and it has a wonderful brassy patina from years upon years of use. It has a black nib. The pen clip broke off in 2024 (I think). I have tried many other pens since getting it, and the only reason I have tried this other pens is because I am now deathly afraid that one day I will lose or break the pen I have been faithfully using as my main driver for the last 7 years, and the emotional vaccuum it leaves behind will be incredibly difficult to fill.
This brings me to what I actually want to write about: I have a deep, appreciative, and loving relationship with this pen, not due to the fact that it is a good pen, but because I have written so much with it, and it has been through so much with me. My opinion has become that this is why my particular Sheaffer Predule is special. It is not just that the pen has survived what I estimate to have been 1.7 million words of writing (that's around 30 192-page notebooks at around ~300 words per page), but that because of this, it is a higher quality pen than the pen I started with, and not just because it's 'broken in'.
I read Pirsig's 'Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance' in the middle of my junior year of college, where he supposes that beyond the definition of most words, making the effort to define 'quality' is an increasingly impossible task. Unfortunately, I am one who is given to impossible tasks; that year I handed in a hand-written essay in response to an Anthropology Midterm that wasn't directly responding to the prompt offered in which I wrote out exactly what I suppose to be my definition of quality: quality is the history behind an object.
The basic treatise following my definition was this: a high quality object is something that has, in some form or other, embodied the story of how it came to be that object. I want to eschew the clinical definition of quality meaning craftsmanship because I do think the two are distinct. I think it is possible for a person to own something cheap and badly made and for it to have higher quality than something comparable that is more expensive and better made provided the relationship between the person who owns the object is more pronounced than that of higher percieved craftsmanship. It's for this reason that I think we find it difficult to detach ourselves from things that we have had for a long time and that have served us well - these things have taken more quality than they had when we first procured them. There is a floor to this, of course, in which a possessed object can be of such cheap materials and craft that being presented with an object with any level of substantially better mixture of the material and craft immediately outweighs the relationship of an owner and the object, but only if the owner feels that way. If not, the cheaper thing is harder to give up, and is as a result subjectively higher quality, and (as Pirsig would agree, I think) quality can only be subjective.
I could give you an example here, but I'm sure it was really hard for you to buy a better coffeetable than the one you lugged around from apartment to apartment for ten years until the exact moment where you realized what a piece of shit it was relative to something that dramatically outweighed your relationship with it and poof, the coffeetable's relationship with you is broken. As a counter, someone comes to your house and says 'your coffee table is a piece of shit' before you have the money to get one, but you immediately reflect on the fact that you lugged it around for 10 years, rolled joints on it, hit your shin on it while falling off in the course of making out with the first love of your life, and that would make it higher quality. We develop relationships with things, and I think it is the constellation of relationships of subjects with objects that give objects their quality.
What this means is that the reason an object of higher craftsmanship than the shitty coffee table (such as a substantially better coffee table) is so obviously the case is because we immediately think of the prior relationships the object has to the subjects that created it; a shitty coffee table from Ikea was produced at a factory by a machine, a higher-quality, woodworker-made coffee table was made be a person, and we intuitively can understand the relationship between that object, the person who made it, and the fact that it has been passed on to us as a record of their work, causing us to have a relationship with the object that might lead us toward caring for the object a lot more.
Now that I have sufficiently exhausted rewriting the gist of the exact essay I think I wrote for that mid-term (I did not intend to, but I did, I am sorry), maybe you can understand why this pen matters to me.
In the years since I established this definition for myself, I promptly forgot it. The reason is that I was broke, and I could not afford to care about things, other than those things I could barely afford and to which I built a relationship. Whether or not this is the 'correct' definition of and treatise for quality has never mattered too much to me as the fact that I took (and I think all of us have taken) it to be the case. I have owned lots of 'things' since I read that essay, and when I had the money to buy things that would last longer, I started buying them, strictly because they would last longer.
In particular, the pen I wrote that essay with for that particular Anthropology mid-term was a black plastic fountain pen that I stole from the University of Georgia Student Bookstore repeatedly because I wanted to write with a fountain pen. The reason I wanted to write with a fountain pen was not because I was, yes, the person who walked around campus barefoot with an Egyptian merchant's hat on my head and a leatherbound notebook in my hands1, but because I did actually grow up somewhere that taught me how to write with a fountain pen. The reason I stole the pen (repeatedly) was because a) I kept breaking them because they were really shitty pens and b) fuck the University of Georgia Student Bookstore. I would open the packaging, remove the pen, and leave, no one the wiser, free to use the pen until the nib eventually folded on itself and stopped writing, leading me, again, to go steal another one from the store. I think I did this four time over my tenure.
After I left school, I think I was able to keep using that particular 'Pen and Ink' (yes, that was the name of the brand) fountain pen before the complete crap aluminium nib shifted and ink stopped flowing. For years thereafter, I began a search for the ideal cheap pen, cycling through the Zebras and Uniballs (I am sorry, I absolutely HATED the Uniball Vision Needle that everyone wanted me to use) until I landed on the Uniball Roller Eco, which I wrote with for several years. The reason? It was unassuming, wrote (fairly) well, was cheap, and the pen clip was narrow and thin enough that I could usually assault the spine of my moleskines, then shinolas with the obligation to let me slot it in.
Unfortunately, one particular niggling aspect of the Uniball Roller Eco is that it is a piece of shit, weighing nothing, and requires remarkably substantial continuous pressure over long writing period from one's hand in order to remain consistent. The other niggling aspect of the Uniball Eco that is true of a lot of cheap things: when you buy lots of a cheap thing so that you don't care that much when you lose it, you eventually hit a wall where you are so annoyed with the fact that you have purchased so much of that cheap thing instead of buying one of a nice thing for the same price of all of those boxes that you decide, you know what, I'm going to just go ahead and buy the nice thing, and because it's a nice thing, I'll have to care where it is instead of continuously turning to look for a pen when you wanted to write something down only to discover that it has magically disappeared, shrugging your shoulders, and moving on.
And so, it was 2020. I rode my bicycle from Brooklyn to The Fountain Pen Hospital on Warren Street in lower Mannhattan, having decided on the pen using the Sheaffer website after two weeks of research and having called them to check if they had it in stock. I knew nothing about fountain pens other than the fact that I liked writing with them, that my last fountain pen was sort of horrible, cheap, and overpriced (again, a very hearty FUCK you, UGA Student Bookstore). The woman who sold the pen to me had the most Long Island accent a reader can dream up, didn't seem to comprehend the momentousness of the event at hand, selling it to me and turning around to walk back over to the Montblancs.
Now, of course, there is a breaking-in period with fountain pens. I didn't know that, so I thought, a week into writing with it and it started to 'railroad', that I'd gotten a lemon. Railroading3 is when part of the line you are writing ends up with a void in the middle, but the ink still flows around this void, leaving parallel tracks. Disaster.
Unfortunately, this happened at a time where I had left New York on assignment, so I couldn't go back to the store during the return period and beg them to give me a new one. The other option I had was to come back from that assignment and ask Sheaffer to warranty the pen, since this is something that was offered by the manufacture - but I was afraid they might not let me do that so soon after getting it, or that they might only give me one warranty replacement, and I didn't want to burn that so soon after getting it.
What that meant is that somewhere in the middle of that assignment, I had no option but to keep writing. This is where I learned the great, magical thing about fountain pens: the problem did fix itself, and the railroading behavior decreased with time. There is a less-than-magical (but still wonderful) reason for this, which (as I mentioned) is that fountain pens break in with use: they are often tipped with a pliable metal that wears as you write with them until it matches your particular writing style. It's not the case that I realized this, necessarily, because what I thought instead was 'fuck you pen, I paid for you, so you are what I'm writing with now', and as the behavior slowly vanished my awareness, I began not to think about the pen at all when I wrote, and only when I wasn't. The pen became an extension of my hand, and I wrote with tremendous ease, and I forgot that the pen was there.
The other thing that happened was that I developed an anxiety about the pen that was exactly what I'd been missing with all of the cheap Uniball Roller Ecos; I was now consistently aware of where my notebook was were specifically because my pen was in it, and the two of them together were of high value2 to me. I couldn't afford to buy another pen, and I didn't want to.
Unfortunately, two months into owning the pen and having broken it in, one day the cap liner became removed from the body of the cap, and the black nib of the pen would no longer stay inside of the cap when I pressed them together. I was devastated, and I emailed Sheaffer directly, asking them to warranty the pen. It turned out the email didn't work, so I called them and they very kindly shipped out a warranty replacement for me, asking me to send in the old pen in the same box of shipment. When the new pen arrived, the nib in the box was silver, rather than the black that I'd been writing with, and I'd just gone through that painful breaking-in period and I liked the black nib better, so I put the old cap on the new pen, then shipped off the two together.4 An interesting side-effect of this that I only found out while writing this essay is that the pen that they shipped me was actually not the same pen model. The black nib is what identified my pen as the 'PVD' model of the Prelude, and they had shipped me the 'Standard' model. What this means is that the cap I have on my pen is NOT the fancy PVD cap (boo) but the rest of the pen is.
This new hybrid pen and I continued a long, close relationship wherein notebook upon notebook were filled, chronicling a change of career, new responsibilities, new relationships, and so on. At some point, the pen clip removed itself from the body after having been stressed to its mechanical limit from being jammed into too many moleskine spines, and I coped with the sharp fangs left behind that wore down into the nubs the pen has today.
I don't know exactly when it was, but at some point in the fortuitous journey of increasing my net worth through a change of career, I realized that I could probably buy another, better pen, or at the very least a companion to this pen, and so I began trying out others. The interesting thing is that I have hated literally every other pen I have purchased because my Prelude is broken in and I love writing with it. However, this fact terrifies me, because I have now written so much with this pen that the prospect of having to find a replacement feels more like an obligation than it does something I am actually interested in doing. I find myself frequently thinking that I need to 'retire' the pen, constantly steeled against a certainty that lives in the back of my mind like crouched gargoyle staring at me from the shadows, telling me that one day, I will lose the pen and never get it back.
I should be clear I have actually lost this pen before. I have the vague recollection of having lost it with my passport and my notebook to the back of an airplane seat sometime just after the pandemic made flying okay again, and it was the only time a notebook I lost ever made its way back to me. I left it on the table at Little Grenjai in 2025 and the Kiwi woman who worked behind the counter set it aside to make sure it got back into my hands. Most recently, I was on a date with my current partner, who I wasn't yet dating at the time, and both watched the pen roll off the edge of a table at a restaurant. My being a gangly fool whose response to things falling is not to immediately reach for them because that is likely to exacerbate the falling by launching the falling thing further into obscurity, instead having programmed myself to watch and listen for where something falls - we watched it roll toward the drain, causing my now partner to leap from her seat and grab it before it fell through the stormdrain. This is one of many reasons I am with this woman today.
The body of the pen is 'brassed' to hell. In fountain pen circles, this denotes a 'well-loved' pen. It is so; I love this pen more than I probably should, a dangerous, unrelenting love that hopes feverishly never to culminate in loss. A love that is the reason I plugged in my laptop 5 hours ago and kept writing.
It's possible that this is how 'reviewers' are made - one acquires an understanding of what a good 'thing' is and as a result go about trying to compare everything to it so that they can relive the experience of having loved something. It's probably the same reason that people immediately 'get another dog' when their dog passes, because it's a desire to fill a hole that opens up when you build a relationship with something that isn't you. The quality of the things in our lives increase as we build relationships with them. Quality is what allows us to appreciate the space that something takes up in our lives and the relationship we have to it, whether it is because we understand the life that made the object, or because we lived with it. It is what compels us to care about things, or for them, whether they are sentient or not. I look at my pen as the time that I have spent writing, maybe more than I do the words themselves, and it shows. We don't get many things in life to care about at that level, because we caring creatures don't live that long.
The point is this: don't go out there looking at reviews expecting that anyone will do what I am trying to do here, because it's probably not the same thing. I think most reviews or testimonials are an attempt to serve as a starting point for the relationship you are going to have with whatever you're researching. What I am telling you is that the quality of what you are allowing to enter your life is shaped by how you build a relationship with it, and I am begging you to do so.
I'll close with this: there is a video of a woman ridiculing her husband because he had reached the end of a spool of wire he had purchased in high school. She doesn't understand the relationship between he and the spool of wire, but he does. I don't care for the social commentary that surrounds this on the internet about their relationship, because it is a question of answering and understanding one thing: the quality of the wire did not matter when he bought it. the quality mattered when he reached the end of that spool, and was faced its loss, a tiny perforation threading the wire through each object into which he had placed it, far into the past, to the moment he began his relationship with the object.
