On intention

1000.013 · 1000 living and loving · essay · 2025.03.10

More intention, or rather, the subject of the understanding needfully described as intention.

We are more than the sum of our parts - I expect that this is true, and the investigation intot he aspects of the self that we use to define this sort of commitment to self-discovery often seem to forget that the narrative of exploration is rooted most in the exploration of a vast inner landscape far outstripping our ability to explore it.

When we contend with this being true, we also account for the fact that in our own definitions or those that we use to articulate a pain of being (often referred to as ennui) we are placing ourselves on an axis of between the conscious and the unconscious and necessarily forcing ourselves to reconcile them; the felt sense of the body when there is something "wrong" contrasted with the centers of logic we use to sense_make_ of the experience equally conspire often to be at odds.

It's for this reason that somatic work has a particular importance that outweighs the requirement to 'make sense' of things rather than simply 'having your senses at your disposal' (Gregori 2004) in order to better navigate the complexity of our animalistic sensations and the higher-order explanations we have for intellect (if, at any juncture, defined by the intersection of a predator and its prey and the race conditions that lead to preparedness for victory on either side). Consciousness is thus an artifact of extrapolation and its tangential importance to the experience of responding to one's conditions.

This is why I generally reject the notion of a 'trauma-informed practice' because the very experience of being even a semi-conscious creature is to have built a network of responses to one's conditions and to have built a foundation on these responses. 'Trauma' negatively ascribes intention to mere 'experience' specifically in order to impose an axis between positions of empowerment and disempowerment - while it's a useful notion in order to better understand this axis, it's only as useful as being 'informed' actually results in progress rather than recalcitrant possession of an inescapable victimhood rejecting the notion of this very same progress.

I part with the notion of examining collective 'trauma' short of requiring oneself instead to 'start with trauma' in order to evaluate ways of progressing past its influence; if one is unable to do so, one is doomed not to resolve a sense of agency leading out of the teleological mire. Progressivism of this sort feels at best to root itself in this lack of agency, placing the burden of action entirely on those that cause the trauma without taking a declarative stance on arbitrating inaction on the part of those with the power differential. Those with the will to overcome an otherwise unconditional surrender to external influence are those who can overcome this sort of teleology; while it may be more difficult, it is nonetheless important to work the fields so that our children can work the shops, so that their children can make art: conditions of liberation are a product of painstaking commitment to action, not throwing one's hands up in the face of the effort needed.

One exception to this (or rather one important step on the journey toward embodying agency) is to ensure that at the least the history preceding one's liberty to progress is made legible and interpretable - its erasure constitutes a doubling back in rejection of accountability. It is possible to reconcile the concept of situational advantage and its lapses with a doxa of agency and an overall increase in degrees of possibility experienced by people from radically different backgrounds.

This reconciliation is not, however, one of meritocracy as the more rationally-minded might clai, Any truly 'meritocratic' function requires a deep removal of any and all biases dwelling in orbit around a given decision. (Of course, if you don't believe that these biases exist... You have a completely different problem).

Finding oneself at odds with the liberal imperative of increasing the index of possibility through a patchwork of non-conciliatory actions such as access programs was a good first step in experimentation, but these efforts lead to a dead-end when there is no conscientious notion of what it means to restructure the system that causes something like 'inequity' to arise in the first place. The importance of understanding that the experiences leading toward something as mundane as a name excluding you from candidacy for work, for example, is an important acknowledgment, because it is then blithely transparent to anyone executing on negative tendencies to assume much of one's identity leaves bare that the decision was not made on merit but rather bias; this specific example does the best to articulate where merit and bias intersect due specifically to the intermediate relationship between the two resulting in one informing the other.
