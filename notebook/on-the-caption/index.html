On 'the caption'

1055.008 · 1055 critique · essay

I think I would like to get really really good at something. Meaning: I would like to learn what it takes to become someone that people see as being very good at something. I got there with photography and while I do think that was an important medium to explore, I do think it was not sufficient for me.
In part I feel this way because in Sontag's Regarding the Pain of Others, she addresses the importance of the caption. She says, in the midst of a paragraph where she divides the use of the caption by the impact of the image, showing that the image does not matter when it is placed in absentia of its context.
"To the militant, identity is everything. And all photographs wait to be explained of falsified by their captions. During the fighting between Serbs and Croats at the beginning of the recent Balkan wars, the same photographs of children killed in the shelling of a cillage were passed around at both Serb and Croat propaganda briefings. Alter the caption, and the children's deaths could be used and reused."
In explicit terms, an image of a child dying or dead does not matter in terms of the emotions it creates in a viewer unless that viewer has an understanding of what they are witnessing: a mutilated child is placed in the abstract can elicit emotion, but it is not emotion tied to any one event. A mutilated Gazan child is another story.
I think it is for this reason that I found it hard to sit with photography as a compelling medium. Likely it is because I have spent so much of my career trying to pry apart the meaning of a photograph on its own, hardly having paid attention to the caption, despite Sontag's counsel. I read this excerpt in Sontag many long years ago, and it means so much more to me now because I have pivoted from pure representationalism within art toward the importance of an artistic context. I'd argue, rather forcefully, that many photographers in the modern age have fallen victim to this - the caption gives context when we are flitting through a language of images, but it does not give meaning. What give meaning is narrative, or 'story'.
I discuss this with friends in private - that we think art is somewhat devoid of meaning in an era that allows art to be generated with abandon, and that because of this artists who create with the desire to imbue their works with meaning are found not to be palatable by the wider market. I find this to be true; it is art without caption - however I do think that the idea of the 'caption' exists beyond the bounds of that which we choose to describe as simply words below the page. It is the context itself, that within which any art or expression exists in order to confer upon that art meaning; the same is true of history and its impact on the medium, It is less that caption is that which makes the image important and more that caption confers upon the image its context. What is context?
In Spain, my partner and I go to see Joan Miró's permanent exhibits at the Fundació, and our (maybe my) impression is that his work must exist in the context of the history that creates it. I stand in front of Miró's 'Painting on White Background for the Cell of a Recluse', where he sat with the canvas for weeks before deciding how to strike the line across the panels. There is another where he did the same, but there was as well a red, a blue, and a yellow splotch in the painting. Miró is the perfect example of the artist whom others may think representationally is not one who had skill, but once one understands the full breadth and constraint required to do what he did, it becomes obvious that anyone with that impression is wholly mistaken. Particularly in its context, Miró's work exists by requirement in the context that produced it. The form of expression he chose as a skilled artist resulted in work that reflected the time that caused him to create the art, and it is in dialogue with the time in which it was created. This cannot be overstated, because without this context, the art may feel good, but it has no valence.
Valence is probably important to define, here - because it's a chemistry term rather than one used in the production of art, but I think it maps. Valence is the degree to which the relationship between what is being witnessed and what actually happened are made visible to the viewer. The art I created never really held valence specifically because I stopped short of giving my art the context it needed for it to have a relationship with the narrative I was hoping to translate.
A friend of mine recently had an exhibit of their own, commendably presented in a public forum at the center of many other exhibits. I'm excited for them, and the images are very good. I can tell the body of work is going to do very well, in the traditional sense. But while it was poignant and the captions were well written, I felt the presentation of the narrative was insufficient; the captions did not do what we need them to as an observer. I was not moved. I don't know if he had the chance to talk about the images and why they are important.
This is where I diverge with Sontag - I do think that while the caption is important, the narrative and method of presentation for the context that they offer is doubly so. Captions without the effort to seat themselves within narrative lack valence. When we as viewers can sense the valence between what we are consuming and what we are meant to feel, we are moved; we can sense the meaning behind what we are witnessing. The ability to sense meaning in an image due to the valence offered by its attachment to context is what probes deeper into the emotional landscape of how we overlap our own experiences (emotions and their triggers) and the experiences of others (eliciting similar emotions, but for different reasons).
As an example, there is a difference between reading something in a book that has images (in which the images supplement the writing) and a photo book. A photo book or a monograph of an artist's work on a subject matter is inherently vain, but that vanity has it's own motive, and I think is what I find detestable about the caption when used not to motivate the viewer toward understanding and caring about the subject, but instead only to share a perspective. Sharing only a perspective without an opinion attached to it is, in its own right, weak. It's, per Kundera, 'graphomanic'. A writer who uses their images to supplement the work that they are doing not with the desire to compel one or all people toward action but rather simply to 'tell on their lives' are not giving their images this desired valence, rather they are engaging in an indulgent act.
I am not arguing that this should be avoided, to be clear - there is importance to the manner in which the caption is brought to someone's attention. Meaning - I think it's important to examine how a caption is used to invoke meaning in the context of a photograph. If Sontag is right, the caption is necessary because it's meant to draw a viewer into the story that the image is depicting.

  • Sontag's claim: the caption determines what an image means
  • Your extension: the caption is necessary but insufficient — narrative is what actually confers meaning
  • Define valence: the degree to which the relationship between what is witnessed and what produced it is made visible to the viewer
  • Miró as proof that context transforms perception (skilled artist whose work is meaningless without the history that produced it)
  • Your friend's exhibit as proof that absence of narrative flattens perception (good images, good captions, but no valence)
  • The personal pivot: why photography felt insufficient as a medium — it made it too easy to stop at the image
  • The implication: in an era of abundant images and scarce context, the artist's real work is building the narrative, not making the image
