I hate Sam Kriss (draft)

1055.007 · 1055 critique · essay · 2026.05.26

You know what would be cool? If a person could hold a device up to their face and speak with abandon, moving through the mildly connective fabric of their thoughts in order to tease out a meaning, then tell their device to arrange those thoughts spoken into an essay that others might read, taking out all the difficult intermediate steps of what makes writing an essay so hard, then post it to social media and manage all the posting that is also so hard, and then, finally, all the critique that comes with having released a thought into the world - which is also so, so hard. That would be great, wouldn't it? To have optimized all the thinking out?
A couple of days ago, as though the poles reversed, I found myself thinking that I no longer want to use AI to check my work or any of my writing, ... or any of my thoughts, either - because I think I finally understand the itching sensation at the back of my neck that feels wholly unsatisfied by AI's claim to be a digital mirror. I'd been using Claude for that purpose, and now I get why I can't - it can't give me what it is that I'm actually looking for, because what I'm really looking for is validation.
The reason was in part the fact that while reading an article by Sam Kriss, I realized that he was going to kill me for doing what I've been doing. I immediately threw up my hands - I don't want to die!I don't really use Claude to write, necessarily. I use it to convert my handwritten writing into text (it's remarkably good at that) and I use it to... shamefully, make myself feel better about my writing. I use AI for validation that what I'm saying is something that anyone might read.
I'm realizing that writing and reasoning is exactly the wrong place for a tool that strings together ideas without really understanding what a writer is trying to get at, or without actually paying attention to whether or not there's a connection in the first place (fun fact, there's a name for that). I'm done doing that, because it doesn't lead me anywhere. Despite those who created AI hoping that it can do so. the sort of reasoning AI is capable of is the sort of reasoning required for mechanism - an answer to a question that is generated by a pattern. That is where it serves the purpose, and I think it's very good at that - good enough that
What this means, to me, is that Sam Kriss probably won't come to my house to kill me, or at the very least won't stand in my living room shouting a tirade of well-phrased cwitticisms (I'm reaching) about how it is that writing is holy and important and deserving of protection. I already get it, and he's right to post a reactionary screed designed to cause outrage. We are outraged. That's the point. Part of this is that I can tell, from reading his words, that he's a human, because he holds positions that I disagree with, and in fact that I hated.
I hated the fragmented reasoning in the middle of Kriss' screed when he says of writer Hanania that "whenever I see anything from Richard Hanania I’m seized by an overwhelming desire to demolish his house", "because I'm Jewish and he's Palestinian". I hated it because it felt human, because I could imagine a person at the other end of the digital tangle sitting in front of their computer and dreaming up the most rage-inducing sentence he could come up with, and because it's the whole reason the essay works.
It is, of course, the exact section that has been clipped and reposted in the day or so since he posted what he did, exactly because it's a horrid thought, and because the circular reasoning that makes it simultaneously anti-semitic and racist against Palestinians is exactly the sort of wordsmithy that causes anyone to stop and contemplate a little longer reasoning period Claude cheerfully puts below a query. I'm still thinking about it, and I read it yesterday, turning it over and over in my head because it was designed to make me angry and because everything else Kriss says in the essay is compelling enough to go back and read again; I excitedly want to express that I do, in fact, appreciate that a horrid opinion about how to treat a group of people is something that makes his writing feel human.
The humanistic impulse in me to say that the two things are not connected--that belonging to a faith shouldn't mean that he wants to commit xenocide--, are the nuclear missile that Kriss is sending to my house from across the internet. It might have been a self-deprecating joke, but it was a bad one designed to be bad, and that's great, and that's my point. Slow clap, it worked. The rest of the essay is fantastic, for the same human, angry reason that jumps out of the text and that I, for one, am still dwelling on. I've already seen any number of rage-fueled takes to the fact that he wrote those words for us to consume, but hey, at least it was human.
This is not an essay against AI, necessarily. I don't want to it to be (I don't even know if it's an essay), but it might end up that way. It seems stupid and ill-conceived that I feel compelled to bring it in to the discussion, because maybe there really is a compulsion within all of us to comment eternally on the present moment.
I know that those among us who care about the sanctity of the word should never hazard to touch the stove on which the kettle is whistling just to see if it's hot - not because I think it's normal to touch the stove, because we're purists and idealists and all the other lists that we use to make ourselves up, but I'm also a realist. I know that sometimes the intrusive appel du vide shows up unbidden and asks us if maybe we should try touching the stove because there may be a quantum chance that just this once it won't be too hot to touch. It's the reason that I do use AI for other purposes - I can tell there's a place for it, and I do use it that way - it feels like a superpower to use it in ways I am undereducated to do otherwise.
However, I am realizing that AI's utility does not belong among my words, my thoughts or anything that urges me toward presentiment and experience and tries to tease out meaning. It's not me doing performing the action and hating the process, it's a machine that I want to do the hard part for me. I am not an AI apologist, because I don't give a shit what you think about me, so I'm not going to describe myself that way. These are just words I'm using to navigate a thought. That's what writing is, after all.

See, readers and writers fall in love with sentences. It's not the paragraph or even the arc of the novel, the essay, whatever, that attracts us. It's the reason that one sentence stood out to me from the many other very good ones Kriss wrote in an essay that had nothing to do with the core subject matter. Most good thoughts are an attempt to reach across the blackness of space between ideas and draw out inference from their connection, and then the hope is that by having drawn that connection, we can derive meaning from the pattern we see connecting them. A good sentence is a window into how a writer sees the world - but there's a thing about windows; if we know that people are going to be looking in, we arrange the room with this knowledge in mind. We use our taste (hah!) to decide what we want people to see in that room and how we want it to be received by them. A good sentence is and act of synthesis, compressing a body of knowledge into a pane of glass.
Every stereotype of a tech bro is a mattress on the floor because no one will ever see the inside of their mind and the machine we are building is more important than our wellbeing. That's the reason the room we're looking at doesn't look like anything, why the takeout boxes haven't been thrown out, and what justifies the impulse to throw this entire fucking essay in the trash before publishing it.
We cannot expect words to do this on their own, even if we draw a semantic map of the likelihood that one word will be connected to another, and then use that map to automate the performance of connection. Part of the promise of AI is that it can do this - that the semantic map will somehow generate the patterns we seek. In some circumstances, it works -- but when we're trying to come up with something that matters to us and outsourcing the reasoning to a pattern generator, it's really just graphomania. It doesn't mean anything specific, because it was written by no one, for no one, from no one's perspective, with the mixed expectation that an unknowing public will read it.
Graphomania as a condition was described long before Milan Kundera asserted that it is a symptom of a society too docile and comfortable to do anything other than write and believe that their writing is worth reading. I've been realizing that people have fallen out of knowing the term - but there's even a clinical definition that comes from having one's temporal lobe affected - an affliction that causes words to spill forth from someone's hand and onto the page without actually reaching a point.
I have a long history with that particular fragment of The Book of Laughter and Forgetting - it was what caused me spend the last fifteen years thinking I couldn't write because my impulse to write was really just a manic impulse. I spent fifteen years mulling over the impact of those few sentences in order to conclude that I fucking hate Kundera for communicating an egotism that delineates the difference between writing with an impulse to explain something and writing because one has an impulse to write.
