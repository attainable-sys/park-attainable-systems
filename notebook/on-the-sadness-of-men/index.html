On the Sadness of Men

1055.006 · 1055 critique · essay · 2026.01.01

Last night, New Year's Eve, my close friend Lauren and I met a man who was out to drinks on his own. It was a celebration of the new year, we had just come from another overly fancy cocktail bar that thought too much of itself to a neighborhood haunt with decent latin techno spinning on LPs, and we met the man just after we came in, ordering whiskey sours at the bar. This sort of thing happens to Lauren a lot when we are out - she is both conventionally and startlingly uncommon in her attraction - a woman exhibiting all the traditional-seeming traits of femininity and all the musculature of a woman who believes in shaping her body into that befitting an Amazon raised on Mount Olympus - a Greek goddess I get to call my best friend.

The man she was speaking to immediately honed in on this 'difference' - it's a line I have heard many men express in our presence, and as I ordered the drinks, I leaned over, cracked my own smile, and was introduced to him (for the purposes of this story, his name will be Morris - not his actual name). He mentioned to the two of us that he could tell we weren't regulars to the bar, that we didn't seem like the usual white crowd that occupies the space. I've always appreciated when people say this to me, because I am mixed, nearly ashamed (and dealing with the shame) of my whiteness - it is a high compliment when people who are not white themselves see immediately that both I am not only white but as well that I take pride in the parts of me that make this difference.

It didn't take long for Morris to speak to us about his child through the photo used as the background of his phone: a milk-chocolate skinned young boy with eyes like nebulas. At our expressed excitement, Morris showed us more photos of him, unlocking the screen, opening his instagram, and showing us photos of the child with a short-shorn white woman with the child in her arms - immediately clarifying 'we're on the outs though, we're on the outs'. Lauren and I said more about how beautiful his son was, about how proud we are of the friends we know who have children, a pride that we as women feel in seeing other women take on the challenge of raising people into adulthood. Morris, for his part, turned from the thought, "yeah but I miss this, I miss being out, I miss being at the parties" - his child is one and a half years old. He went on to say that he loves his child, that the boy matters most to him, but he wanted the freedom of being out at the bars on New Year's.

Lauren and I watched Morris move on to other women, making out with a woman he didn't speak a word to on the floor, coming up to me after my asking 'how was that', and him responding 'I wasn't in love with her'. He tried to kiss me downstairs at the bathroom sink, only getting my neck. Desperate.

He tagged on to the next bar as the night began to drag to a close - Lauren and I had wanted to dance, and the floor closed at Doris around 3. We walked to Friends and Lovers, getting to the door around 3:20, where the doorman tried to charge us each a $30 cover, Lauren and I playing an unintentional good-cop/bad-cop to get us all in for $40. Morris tried to buy us more drinks, we denied them, we danced with three other people on the floor until the music died fifteen minutes later. Morris put his hand on the waist of a passing woman who he didn't speak a word to, we watched her lift his grip from her skin, and he sat down at the bar.
The last scraps of conversation between the three of us were in telling him to go home to his son. I reminded him that he probably has less than a year of his child not remembering whether or not he was home for holidays and that this will start to become important soon. The forlorn expression on his face is what we left him with, and as we walked to Lauren's apartment, she mentioned how she felt he was suspect, faithless, her distrust of him framed through the phrases he used. I found him pathetic in a way that I still feel sympathy for.

At the age of 35, I have heard more and more women in my life deride the relationships they have with the men in theirs. My sister says to me (not for the first time) as we visit her over the holidays that were it not for the marriage she is already a part of (to a man she claims truly to love), she would not be dating another man. At a writer's workshop in Mexico, a black woman tells me that she chose to be in a relationship with a man to raise her kids because it's hard enough to be raised black, but not because she wanted to. A close friend's husband struggles with his own freedom to write because they have a child and this incurs responsibilities that he becomes responsible for when he's 'finally off for the holidays', which my friend doesn't have the luxury of getting 'time off' from under her employ as a mother. Another close friend, this one a man, tells me how lost he feels in relationship with his wife, who finally came out to him as both non-binary and queer; it feels like an attack on his masculinity, and they have not had sex in a year because she is protective of her body in a way he doesn't know how to react to - but they are still married, she is still with him.

It could be that all of this is symptomatic of a society slowly learning to deinterlace the relationship between being a woman and the oppressive structures that womanhood places upon us in a world built by and for the advantage of men. Women have seen significant concessions and reformist progress; Sontag says in her essay 'The Third World of Women' that "{a}s otherness is reduced, some of the energy of sexual attraction between the sexes will decline". She wrote this in 1972, and in the same essay she describes the sorts of progress that a patriarchal society will offer to women as concessions, what she called 'reformist' policies of which we should be suspect. We have seen many of these concessions come to pass - our equal-ish access to executive roles in workplaces, a heavily diversified set of roles made available to women, a broader (though shaky) access to abortion, equalized or equal-ish pay. These are all outlined as the sorts offered to women to keep us placated in a society that wants to keep us functioning as a part of the engine.

However, Sontag also balks at any assertion that the gap of equality between the genders (I am conscious that at the time of her writing, speaking of all the genders was not common parlance) should be subsumed under the struggle for freedom from the oppression of class - indeed it is a reformist stance to say that there is 'no war but class' war, and women are offered "merely liberal improvements in their situations" when the struggle against bourgeoise oppression uses the freedom of women as a political tool. The struggle of women, per Sontag, is a longer, deeper question than any of liberalist politic - the separation of the genders and the structures this entails roots beyond class and in a different dimension from that of a liberation from the axis of power; it does not encompass the struggle for collective freedom, because the freedom of women is a question of axial power between the sexes. In a modernized argument, it is one of comprehending concessions to the explicit loss of power under the dynamics of a society structured around men, for men, and by men. She lays a finger on a feeling I witness in men today:
"If women change, men will be forced to change. {...} The structure of society is founded on male privilege, and men will not cede their privileges simply because doing so is more humane or just. Men may make concessions, reluctantly, granting women more "civil rights". In most countries now, women can vote and attend institutions of higher education and are permitted to train for the professions. Within the next 20 years, they will get equal pay for equal work and be granted effective ownership over their own bodies. {...} but these concessions, however, desirable they may be, do not challenge the fundamental attitudes that maintain women as second class citizens nor touch the root of male privileges."
She precedes this by laying claim to the same feminist position I have heard before in the presence of other more consciously-minded women who are aware of the structures against which they abrade, that "it is not for women to assume the task of liberating men, when they first have to liberate themselves. [...] Supposing that these changes can be undertaken in collaboration with men minimizes (and trivializes) the range and depths of women's struggle".

I witness this from the remove of fifty years and the understanding that concessions have been made; we as women in this society do enjoy much more liberty and access to equality, but I broadly agree with Sontag's opinion that "it is about power". My sense is that the concessions of a reformist society have however offered us some remarkable progress in the delimitation of women's agency, and that this is resulting in the threat to the power of men against which current political divides seek to inoculate themselves. Sontag predicted this, she is not the only feminist to have done so, because the convergent evolution of an educated class causes the emergence of collectively held opinion and the structures it begins to build. 2026 has raced far enough into the new millennium for us to understand that we have made progress on the acknowledgement and some degree of reconciliation of how widely splayed are the dimensions of human expression; I would not be writing this so safely were this not to be true. I believe we are, as women, more free, even while freedom is an ur-goal underlying more efforts to be made and is unattainable in its lack of definition; structurally an aspirational lacuna toward which we patiently labor in the manner of Foucault's definition of enlightenment.

I also think that the effects of this slow liberation, even those that exhibit the qualities of 'liberal improvements in (our) situation' make themselves apparent in how women have chosen to express themselves, both in terms of aesthetics and in the teleological assertion of aesthetic as an expression of internalized belief. The photo that Morris had shown us of his wife was one of a woman who had chosen to tacitly reject traditional expressions of gender that define her as feminine - I mentioned her short hair, but both Lauren and I agreed, while laying on the floor of her apartment in the 5am stupor of a night's summary, that were it not for his explicit mention of her as his wife, we might have assumed her queerness. To be clear, she may well be queer, raising a child with a man with whom she is 'on the outs', a man who seems to be reaching for a time in his life where his own freedom was less limited. In the morning (afternoon, really) I sit on my chair opposite the couch and recount the night to my partner, and we conclude that Morris exhibits all the qualities of a man nostalgic for a time where clubbing meant being able to touch women in some presumably benign manner. She explicitly defines his kiss on my neck as assault, I don't disagree, but I found him benign.

I found him benign and further felt so when as we turned the corner toward Friends and Lovers, Lauren immediately dangled a practiced question in front of Morris to see if he would bite, asking him 'what is your relationship to masculinity?' Morris leaned away from us as he walked, not quite stumbling, maybe taken aback, saying that he doesn't think about those things, that he 'doesn't like to get political about things', and I clapped down on the use of this language, saying that it is not a question of politics, rather a question of rights, because we live in a society that has been built around masculinity. The question is as a result not politically charged, but direct, a personal question to someone we want to know more about - do you ask yourself this question? You must. Morris responded by saying he had thought of these things, that his wife is a Columbia graduate, that he himself went to Stanford. My partner mentioned upon hearing this that our interaction over this question sounded like two cats playing with a ball of yarn, and I do think there was a certain cruelty to the question. We didn't get an answer from him before reaching the doorman of F&L.

It could be that the relationship Morris has with with his wife did at some point exhibit some consciousness of how gender and race play into the realized experience of their relationship to one another - it would surprise me if someone Stanford educated would truly feel that avoiding the question of his relationship to masculinity would be due to its 'political' implications, -- but some part of me witnesses in hindsight the use of a vocabulary designed deflect the question. I do not personally believe that he does not have an opinion on the matter, rather my sense is that he feels harmed by the presence of the question and its effect on his existence as a man. It could be (and here I truly cannot make the assertion to this likelihood) that his relationship with blackness and a the painfully slow unfurling of freedom for black men is affronted by the loss power as a man. It could be that this is just as well a worthwhile question to be asking, regardless of whether or not he thought so himself - either way I view his use of a language attempting to deflect the question as one of emotional worry, of a tiredness. As I said, I found him pathetic in a way I felt sympathy for,.

The dynamics of gender and liberation are questions foremost over the concession of power, not concessions of superficial improvements to the surface of an otherwise unchanging and unprogressive society. These are not my words, they are Sontags, but I do believe them to be true enough as proven by the experiences of oppressed peoples seeking their own liberation to warrant speaking them as though they are my own. However, I think we are, today, in revolt. It is the reason that the two most qualified candidates for presidential leadership in the United States have been women, and they have both lost handily to a man who represents the most idealized expressions of gender by men. Power is being threatened. Sontag is right, "Women cannot be liberated without reducing the power of men". Men do not know what to do about this. The first quarter of the New Century was one that offered us the chance to create concessions in the right of race to power; Obama was elected, and the Mechanism has sought to inoculate itself to this transgression. Culturally, this progress still made way for other concessions in the access to power for women, and it turns out this is more threatening, and truly we aren't ready for it. The second quarter of this great Century will continue to ask the question.

Broadly what I witness in women now is an expression of gender as though to reject, much of the time, the signals is broadcasts. I think I witness in men an unmooring in response. I may be overly focused on the expressions Morris used to avoid our rhetorical hook-and-reel, and maybe it was cruel of us in a certain way to pose the question given the security of our temporary friendship to a man who by most accounts we should have found societally reprehensible - out on the town from his wife, leaving a child at home - but I do think there is something to a 'tiredness' I saw in Morris when the question was asked, because I see it in many other men perceiving disabuse of their power by a society attempting to equalize gender.

I see it in my aforementioned friend seeming threatened by his wife's willingness to share the dimension of her sexuality, but Andrea Long-Chu takes this to its extreme in "Females", saying that "if sexual orientation is basically the social expression of one's sexuality then gender is basically a social expression of someone else's sexuality. In the former case one takes an object in the latter case one is the object". I cannot explain why she protects herself from my friend's advances but remains in relationship to him, but I understand why she does because I see in many newly queer people the same foundational rejection of a relationship with the dynamics of gender, expression, sexuality, and the dynamics this produces when too consciously brought into the purview of physical interaction. My friend's wife's hair is also shorn short to her head in a 'pilot's cut'. She is a strong woman, any time I've ever spoken to her. My friend is exhausted and thinks his marriage is failing. Maybe it is, and maybe it is because masculinity does not know what to do under the circumstance that power is ceded to women.

The exhibition we are witnessing in our society of power's concession is different from what Sontag specifically describes as 'liberal improvements', I think. I return to the question of whose duty it is to educate men on what to do when their power is diminished, but men do not know what to do, from what I can tell, They themselves seem quite bewildered by what it means to view women as equals. I describe this feeling on the floor of Lauren's apartment as we close the night, gesturing with my arms to the removal of cobwebs around my fist held like an anchor in the center. I think men are confused by having the lead themselves through the unknown of women's liberation, because women's liberation will leave them behind if they do not work to liberate themselves. If they are not confused, it is because they are rejecting the notion that there is something to work on.
