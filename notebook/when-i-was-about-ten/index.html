When I was about ten

1050.024 · 1050 writing · memoir · 2026.02.07

When I was about ten, my dad purchased a framing machine and put it in his workshop at the deepest end of the two-car garage underneath our duplex in Brameschoff. He placed it conveniently between the working surface of his toolbench and the far right wall of the flourescent-lit space, and its tempting steel-jawed maw reminded me more of a guillotine than it did anything else. The device operated by placing a piece of wood (or, more fantastically "any object" as my father said at the time) along its metal track with the aim of cutting a triangular bite out of whatever was being turned into a frame, then putting one's entire body weight on the steel lever of below its teeth to section out a 90-deg angle. Done correctly and with careful measurement, 'any object' could yield a frame for a painting, a print, or even other objects.
The dream of this device sat in the corner through many openings and closings of the garage door at the opposite end, the sunlight never scraping deep enough into the bright, cavernous space to touch it on even the steepest of summer afternoons. The day it was purchased and explicably became a part of the many objects that followed us from country to country was a freezing one; the incredibly steep driveway that in the summer almost impaled me on my bicycle handlebars when I lost control was an impenetrable icy glissade in the winder. This resulted in a careful navigation of the framing machine's hulking, obtuse frame through the shared foyer of our building, and down the steps to the garage. When installed, I was forbidden to touch it,
My brother and I would spend the beginnings of our adventures in that garage, giving ourselves and our stuffed animals both fake and real piercings with the declaratively scarce supply of split washers. Once, I put the head of my stuffed tiger in the path of the framer's blade, putting my foot on the lever, looking back at my brother with a threat.
We went outside instead, dropping below the tarmac lot that acted as a driveway, down the treachery of wooden, overgrown steps between the holly, through the lawn we never occupied, and into the field adjoining. I lost the first swiss army knife my father had bought me in that field that same summer, a bright red thingj that he called the best knife a person could own. For my birthday, he bought me a metal detector to search for it. I used it once or twice in the field, finding other trinkets, or cans, or nails, but never the knife. We took the detector with us to the beach once, where I used it to find other unnameable objects. Before we moved away, my dad spent two afternoons combing the field, returning far after night had fallen triumphant, placing a now rusted but altogether usable bright red knife and placing it in front of me at the dinner table. I lost the knife when we moved to Italy, and he replaced it with another black one. I never touched the metal detector again, and it disappeared the same way many children's toys steadily disappear from the clearing of clutter at their parents hands.
In Georgia, I used the same black swiss army knife to cut three small horizontal lines on the inside of my left wrist, and when my mother noticed, she made me sleep in the hallway two floors above my room, on the landing between my sister's room, my father's office, and my brother's - in sight of their bedroom door. She confiscated from me, and gave it back a year later. I still find in my cabinets today, from time to time.
The framing device sat in the corner of the garage in Georgia when my dad helped me get my first car. It was a russet Datsun 280z, which he purchased from a man in Gainesville, a town over, who was selling the car to make room for a kid in his life. Gainesville had a large hispanic population; when our dad got home from bringing me that car, he told us the story that the man who'd sold it to him recommended we put a spoiler on it, because then we'd have ourselves a 'Porch'.
I drove that car for a season, where the trebuchet of the car's driveshaft and the uneven weight distribution of its engine relative to the de-upholstering cockpit eventually sent me into a 180-degree spin in the middle of traffic, narrowly avoiding an accident on my way to meet a girl I'd talked to online. Our neighbor offered to buy the car when it was clear to my father that the cognitive overhang of performing the desire to fix up the car was too much for me, while I privately feared appearing too masculine for wanting to do so.
The next car my dad got me was a Mazda 626, which he purchased at low miles from an auction. I'd wanted a Japanese car because they are more reliable. The Mazda 626 was the only model that came with a Ford transmission, which did exactly as I expected by blowing up on me in Valdosta on my first spring break in college, half of the distance down from Forsyth county to see a woman I'd never get to love the way I wanted to and who I love exactly the way I do today.
My parents surprised me with a royal blue Nissan Versa on the day they helped me move out of my freshman dorm, and I scratched the hell out of the right rear door panel halfway up a dirt road in the Cibola National Forest outside of Pie Town, New Mexico three weeks later, five days into a cross-country road trip to Yellowstone with a woman I met on Facebook and whose name I don't remember today. I drove the Versa for another year, driving it back to Yellowstone with three friends in tow for another summer of seasonal work, until on a sleep-deprived drive back from a day of hiking and a night of drinking wine from a nalgene, I drove it into an elk, crushing the front right frame.
I drove it to Bozeman a week later with a single headlight working and a pinch weld and a replacement windshield helped me limp back to Virginia, where the insurance declared the car totaled and gave them enough to lease a Honda CR-V I drove for another year until they closed the lease on my move to NYC.
It was in Virginia, probably six years after college, visiting from New York in a rusted black Tacoma I bought with money from my photography business whose towbar fell off on the highway after a Thanksgiving at Denny's in West Kansas, that I finally helped my dad extricate the framing machine from the fifth workshop it has been a part of, turning it over into the hands of two young men whose faces don't come to mind. I was twenty-seven or twenty-eight, and the framing machine had been a part of my family for more than half of my life. Helping those men lift the awkward imbalance of steel and blades from the basement happened in the midst of navigating what I should and shouldn't be doing as a woman; whether or not despite the influence of hormones on my strength, I should or shouldn't be helping the two strangers lift the weight we had carried with us for seventeen years out of the possibilities my father had seen for himself in his workshops. In the end, I did, and we carried it across the lawn, past the careful tend of garden my mother had remade for six years, and into the bed of their pickup, leaving our lives forever. In all of those seventeen years, my dad never once used it to make a frame.
