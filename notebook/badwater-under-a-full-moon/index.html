Badwater under a full moon

1050.026 · 1050 writing · field note · 2026.07.03

running through the desert under a full moon
badwater is a place
tell me where I got lost
made of something
maybe salt
not white
baked brown by the sun
you aren't special

To run through the desert in March is one thing. To run through the desert in the middle of the night, under the light of a full moon, to turn off your headlamp and let that light guide you, to think that you've died and you're in that middle space, waiting to understand where you need to go next, that is another.
You hear lies from people about which direction you should be headed, but they aren't lies, or rather they aren't meant in bad faith; they become lies because you're tired, because you're in a different state of mind, because you're accusing the desert of taking something from you that you offered, that you gave up. You stand at the edge of Badwater thinking that there was something you could have done different, and where everyone else runs left, you turn right, and then you're alone, except for the few who follow you, who frame the experience in exactly the same terms and sense of self-respect, and you all watch the sun fall and the stars rise and you get lost in it. The roof of the car is a temple. You don't hear a soul headed toward you.
Somewhere on the route south a bolt shakes loose and you see panic on the face of every other runner with you, the feeling of being so far into something that you don't know what you can do but keep going. You trade out teams, you ask one another if you need a break. You look at one another, dogged eyes staring back. No, I want those miles, those are my miles. And so you rotate, and right when you're headed uphill, the night comes.
I didn't get lost in that desert. Not exactly. I tore something out of me that was afraid of getting lost and there was a moment where we came over the ridge and the moon was already there, waiting for us, but the road we came down was so dark it felt like a jagged edge had been pulled away from the sky. We saw one other car, and we never saw their runners, and the hill was steady, and in my miles the car dropped off the horizon, so I was left with a straight line in the dark headed east.
It felt like I'd died. What I mean by this is that there was a moment where exhaustion, the amount of sugars and absorption-optimized carbohydrates in my blood met the fraying wires of electrolytic expenditure in my brain, and I made up shadows following me. I turned off my headlamp all the same. It feels good to die. That's the reason it's so enticing. I wish I didn't feel that way.
I remember in the middle of two miles churned out at a flat pace without a headlight or a soul to see thinking that if purgatory is real, it does feel like this: terrifying, but wondrous at once. Dying while you're doing your favorite thing has its costs. I no longer felt like a person. This is what it's like to di, and I feared it, because that cloud of fog to my left didn't look real, and the sound of the frogs didn't feel real because the desert meant they weren't, because the movement of shadows as I churned forward in a single, unstoppable line meant not to choose death.
By the time you meet up with your friends again you'll remember the same thing, and you'll get to them at the exact moment you need them to remind you that you haven't died. I turned to Steve when I took his hand and I said I wanted to run a mile with him because I was afraid. I'm not usually. But he said sure and we kept going until we got to the RV, and they were all ready, and they took off, and we went to sleep, 50 more miles through the desert at 3 am again and again past people who didn't expect us to be there, spirits or apparitions that didn't trust themselves to exist. We didn't exist. We don't. I did. I'm glad I'm alive, and whatever I threw at the desert was thrown back, that fear turned into a zeal for life. That lie we were told was just a misunderstanding. We're in the right place, at the right time, and that moon, heavy over head, shining light down like a beacon or a gift, well, it never saw us to start with, kid. you're not that special anymore.
