Every day is resistance

1000.012 · 1000 living and loving · essay · 2025.02.04

Every day is some sort of resistance. I just don't really know to what, and I'm conscious of what it means for me to require it.

It could be that in times like this there is a sensation of resistance that follows us, because we see the relative normalcy of whatever it is that we have had to call a life and to see it disrupted is to seek out the logic of its passage from our attention. It's a relatively normal reaction, I realize - to struggle along in your current version of the present and to feel that you should somehow remain in this state, because while it is a struggle, at least it's yours, at the least this struggle is something that you can hold onto in the otherwise crashing and raging flood of change that continuously surrounds our ability to process the present.

It could be that the kind of resistance we have been told to employ is the sort that aims squarely at our expectations of safety rather than the sensation of positive change--I can't say that we expect our present to return to some idyll, but I do know that one core difference between people is the hope for something better against the expectation that things were at some point in the past better in their own right. This latter point feels like a particular crux periculi - when we seek out an idyll in the past, we are cursed never to meet it. The future is far more possible. A hope to make the future more like the past is a hope to return to something that doesn't exist, and indeed likely something that didn't exist in the first place.

My expectation is that this sends a message of hope - we might recognize that there is something far more valuable in seeking out the sort of unity and participation in a joint outcome far outweighing any concept of expectation. When we ask AI what is one thing that humanity could change about its nature in order to inspire forward social progress and it says empathy but somehow the default mode of a collective humanity is to be so fearful of shared power as to believe that this is something of which we aren't capable, something that we appear unable to comprehend the requirements to achieve, nor something that seems so dubiously out of reach due to the amount of social progress required in order to make it happen.

I say this, and I'm aware it's happened before in history, but that the unifying action that made it all possible was central control - there's no time in history where harmony was achieved by equanimity across all classes. There has always been some sort of hard or soft power balance that kept exploitation in check - and this indeed reveals exactly that which causes the mission of unity to fall apart before it is ever seen as achievable. Any wilfully democratic systems seems to fall apart at the moment that there is the affordance for anyone in the system to seize control of it.

This is nothing new, of course - it's something that I can appreciate having been known and part of the reason that many economic and governing systems are built around the principle of oscillating balance, in addition to the same reason that technologists want to build trust-minimized systems, but the former is slow and does not react well to change while the latter handwaves away any requirement to have a system of appeals to justice when a system is revealed to have erred. Neither of these facts negate the importance of figuring out a solution so that there is the ability to do the most important part: coordinate.

Coordination failure is at the root of any time there is deviation from the core principle, and this is truly what most gets in the way of maintaining a self-regulating system. If there is no affordance for a system of maintenance to be put in place, then there is no way to let go of the system once you have pushed it out into the world, and it will always require some form or other of persistent regulatory body that ensures it keeps going. In fact, more than likely, these systems will be exploited specifically by those who have become chained to it because the desire to create a system of liberty and equanimity was not intended to be a system that would also continuously require stewardship by those who create it.

This is central to the problem of coordination systems, particularly those that orient toward its financialization. What has always interested me is not how you pay people to do work, but how you encourage them to work together in the first place - and when we look back through history, it becomes apparent what the most effective systems have been. Great works such as those created by everyone from the Egyptian pharaohs to the Manhattan project dictate that what is most important is to orient a public toward a common goal. With the pharaohs it was the worship of their would-be living gods, with Roosevelt's Civil Conservation Corps it was the creation of a new and better United States with the preservation of its natural beauty as the jewel in its crown, with the Manhattan project it was the urgent requirement to do what was necessary to respond to the fear that someone might find out how to make the bomb first - that someone might hold the bigger stick before anyone else.

In any of these cases, the common goal is the unifying force, but it is never what keeps people working together. Whether the goal is worship, greatness, or fear - the regulatory function that many lean upon is the requirement to either oblige people to the work (mandatory stewardship) or to put in place a system of reward for the work done. Not often mentioned in the face of that which created the Great Pyramids was Egypt's burgeoning middle class those who would spend a portion of their year working on Great Works for the pharaohs in power and be recompensed for this work with their needs being met (not to mention the slaves who were obliged to this work). Nor is it brought up often enough that the regulatory function in place for the Manhattan project was a more global fear of someone else building the bomb, while the more immediate function that inspired those scientists to take part in executing one the greatest human achievements were the parties that took place on that mesa in Santa Fe every weekend.

Coordination requires the same balance of work and recompense that we expect out of the ability to lead stable lives - it is as important to work the fields as it is to sit back and admire the work that has been done, and it is as important to reap a harvest as it is to eat of what you have harvested. This is the root of abundance mindsets in their own right, to understand that the relationship between hard work is the reward of not having to work for periods of time - but there is also the meaningful realization that this is not true of those who fully devoted themselves to the act of creating great things - many of these people work(ed) tirelessly and at a gross personal cost because the goal the work at hand is to create something necessary. We see this in any genre of goal-making and indeed raise the mention of those so devoted, those who spend the hours that many of us would allocate to rest to the tireless pursuit of obligation, or to 'calling'.

I don't know where the thought leads, then, to consider that what unifies people is on the one level ideological belief, but on the other is the requirement by any one person that they lead a life of self benefit. I see polarization where the centrality of one's attention lies - the belief in the scope of where it is that we should be paying attention and those who should benefit most from the joint effort made

I'm trying to get my mind back into a place where I'm not completely at the point of doom and gloom - mostly because I'm not feeling out what it is that I should be doing and instead am feeling as though I don't really have the motivation to continue on with the work that we have been doing. I feel left behind, and I want to go to school, I want to move on with my life. It's not necessarily that I think what I have to do with my time is something bigger and better, I'm just not enthused by the state of things and more than anything I feel as though I'm watching the world around me fall apart in a way that doesn't really make me feel as though I have to give it much attention.

If what I'm really hoping to see is something substantial, my stronger sense is that I have to work within my constraints and I have to build of myself something that I can consider to be important, but only to me. something that I know I can look back on and be proud of. It could be that what I have to do with this is conceive of a framework of some sort for how it is that we build social structures, because I don't think that people have put enough work into defininig these particular parts of what it takes to make people work together.

This is why I'm considering the likleihood of there being something more interesting at the root of all the things that we know we'll see coming ahead. I don't want to be the sort who isoing to believe that what we're going to see is a full collapse - there are some parts of it that I do think are actually good and I think that we'll see something positive come from it, but it really does feel like the cresting of a wave, the sort of things that when looked back on largely benefitted us.
