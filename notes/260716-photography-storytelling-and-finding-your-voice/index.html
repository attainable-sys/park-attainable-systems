Photography, storytelling, and finding your voice

There are three things I'm thinking about today, mostly because I've been trying to figure out what I'm actually doing with my life, but also because I know I'm meant to tell stories.

1. You find your voice by using it

  • getting tied up being perfect about what you post
    • thanks to Caro (@iamcaro)

  • I want to get better at being imperfect, which is why you're seeing my face

2. Stories only become stories if other people repeat them

  • a photographer who doesn't show anyone their photographs is just making things
    • I was that sort of photographer for a long time. I wanted other people to tell my stories for me.
      • that was a mistake
  • 'content' and 'art' are two different things
    • art without the critic is just content
  • 'Content creation' (what I'm doing here) is somewhat distinct from art because it's about attention, not about discourse.
    • I disagree with myself here, though, because again, the point of this specific video is to engender discussion.
    • content is an intentionally neutral term because it doesn't actually want to have an opinion, it just wants to be consumed
    • when we consume things, they are meant to give us energy to do other things
    • when something is truly 'content' it is designed to be popped in your mouth, swallowed, and then the next thing consumed
  • I spent a lot of my career creating images that I thought would stand on their own.
    • What made me sick, and I genuinely mean sick here, was that images don't do that
    • a pretty image doesn't have valence
    • I now realize that images only have meaning if you stand in front of and around them and tell people that what they are looking at is important.
    • Storytelling is about telling stories over and over again until people listen and start repeating them, not telling them once and then being rid of them

3. You should not be trying to make the whole world your audience

  • There's a saying I see a lot on Instagram and Youtube :
    • "If you're seeing this, the algorithm has decided this is for you"
  • This phrase terrifies me, because my creating this, you're not actually trying to cultivate an audience. You're just hoping that some mechanism or other will find that audience for you.
  • if you want an audience, you have to do more than just putting stuff out into the world - you have to sow the seeds of what you want to see and then cultivate what grows into something that has meaning
    • this is what I'm talking about when I mention that your stories do need to be told, over and over, until other people are telling them with you.
  • building an audience means caring about the people you are trying to talk to.
  • I'm not here to crap on algorithms, exactly - I think that work's already been done by others and it's not what I want to talk about
  • however, when we aim for virality, we're not aiming for audience - we're aiming instead for the whole world. when you think your addressable market is the entire world, you'll never be able to imagine the sort of person who will actually want to hear what you want to say

So what does this mean for me?

Let's go back to the image I showed you earlier:
[ image ]

the story behind this image is that I took it in Window Rock, AZ in the middle of covering someone who was cycling across the Colorado Plateau, speaking to land rights and land use advocates.
Specifically, I was covering a hearing that was happening around what was going to happen with the Salt River Generating station in Page, Arizona. This hearing was a part of a series of discussions that were going to help decide whether or not the coal fired power plant would retain its employees under a lease from the Navajo nation, which in turn would keep the significant number of employees of the Diné nation employed. At the time, the power plants in Navajo Nation employed a significant portion of the Diné working population, and the fact that they were being decommissioned in preference for other power sources was going to mean that a lot of people were about to lose their jobs.
On the other side of the table were those who did not want the Diné government to spend money on the lease and instead to spend it on better services within the nation itself.
What this looked like was a collection of those who were employed by the plant and those who did not want the money of the nation spent in this way surrounding the table to discuss the matter, and largely not coming to agreement on what should happen next.
What ended up happening is that the nation did extend a lease on the power plant, which didn't do much to compete with the cheaper power sources outside of the nation, and the power plant was finally decommissioned in 2019, leading to everyone losing their jobs anyway.
To take this photo, I had left the council building where the discussion was being held and walked to this monument that is the reason the town is called Window Rock. At this site, there was a woman walking with her daughter (I remember the daughter's name, it was Aurora). In the seconds I shot the photo, someone appeared up on top of the rock and she waved at them. I'd asked her if she knew the person and she said that she didn't - and what I felt like I caught in this moment were two people who didn't really know one another having a connection over a long distance.
To me, that was a sort of message. It's a story of reaching across a divide - a hole in the sky between two people in a period of change - and trying to connect anyway.
The issue is that I didn't really push on these images after I took them. I sort of thought that they would stand on their own - up to that point they were the most compelling work that I'd produced, and it was also the last real documentary story I shot before taking a step back from the process.
