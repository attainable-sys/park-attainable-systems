On my run yesterday, I realized that what matters most to me right now is that I actually get my shit together about writing - at least in the sense that I want to take this seriously  and approach it with the deliberation I have been seeking out in the practice. It's mostly been out of a desire to see myself get better at it - up to this point in my life, people have always told me that they would read what I have to write. I've never been sure if this is the right motivation.

I'll explain a bit - in 2010 I was in a riverboat with twelve or so other people on my way to Rurrenabaque, Bolivia to spend time in the woods pretending to be a naturalist. I still have the notebook I brought with me where I sketched the native plants and their relationship with nature. One among a few books I brought with me was Milan Kundera's "[[The Book of Laughter and Forgetting]]", which would be the second Kundera book I had read.

While we cut through Amazon headwaters at sunset, or midday, (I can't remember, but it was beautiful), I opened Kundera and read through Part Six, where Kundera seats his description of laughter as the sound of the soul disagreeing with the state of reality, or rather specifically that "laughter can be a revolt of the spirit against reality as it is", where angels laugh because everything makes sense, but devils laugh because none of it does. We were on the boat for hours. My memory of what else he said isn't linear, because it was also this day that I read what he says before this, in Part Four, which was less aesthetic, but prevented me from writing for so much longer.

In Part Six, our two-dimensional woman protagonist is exhausted and ineffectual, and her friend Tamina arranges for her to meet with a local novelist Banaka to discuss her desire to seek in herself whatever it is that compells people to write. To the revolting novelist Banaka she says "I have a feeling that my experience inside is worth writing about and could be interesting to everybody". Banaka rebuffs her by decrying the act of writing novels as 'deeply narcissistic' and not a means through which people come to understand themselves and others.

I remembered this passage incorrectly for the better part of a decade because I shelved the Aaron Asher translation and edition of the book that I picked up from some bookstore in Buenos Aires in a book exchange in some other part of South America and the cover, a photograph of a mirror on a table angling sunlight toward the other half of the same table, revealing a coffee cup, was a beautiful edition that apparently is only sold in the Southern Hemisphere and I've never wanted to own the garish Harper Collins Perennial edition of the book. The way I remembered this passage both smeared with the following passage--in which Kundera recounts a ride with a taxi driver in Paris--and my own memory or interpretation of what Banaka says to Bibi. In my memories, Banaka is arrogant in his response, but Bibi comes off as bumbling herself. Maybe I was a misogynist at the time too. I thought the point was that Bibi was mundane, and that she was wrong, that what her "experience inside" was meaningless and lacking in importance, and that no one would want to read it, just like no one would ever want to read what I had to write.

The following passage that Kundera did write and that I have now extricated from my memory by verifying it against the original text is an observation by Kundera that his taxi driver, who writes and fills "notebook after notebook" with his writing out of reaction to his insomnia, is not writing for anyone that he knows, not to be understood by friends or his family, but for a "public of unknown personas". This action is seen as tragic - we have no way of knowing if the taxi driver's writing is any good, and never having shared his writing (indeed we might think that he is writing so that these notebooks may only be read when he dies). It becomes an open question as to what end and why the taxi driver should even be writing, or for whom. If writing is performed for this unknown public, it is not possible to ever feel the value of the work--but the mere act of seeking to write so that this 'general public' *might* read what you have written is deeply narcissistic, per Kundera. Who on earth would care about who you are and your stories? Who would care enough to read them?

Kundera doesn't argue that true novelists like Banaka are above this reproach - in fact all that separates them is a sense of aesthetic sensibility - , but the narcissism of writing without any consideration of who will read it among this undefined public constitutes instead a 'graphomania' - a believe that no matter the context, someone will read it. Who? Who on Earth?

It is this pair of passages, smeared through my personal history, which delayed for over a decade any inclination I had to write for anyone, either as a practice or otherwise. I was not narcissistic, you see - and I could not think of who would read what I have written. In the decades since first reading it, I stopped short of thinking that writing was for me anything more than a practice of remembering. I considered it an act more of coping, a method for navigating anxieties - writing for no one to be read by no one, and in many cases writing was so deeply personal to me I would find myself myself going back and scoring through pages of written text in the hopes that I could erase conclusions, revelations, unbounded truths that I was not yet equipped to handle, maybe because, as Andrea Long Chu writes in "Females", I actually only exist in response to what the world expects of me, maybe because I what I didn't want to conclude at the time was that I am a woman, and I was female, despite all insistence to the contrary at the time.

I don't know fully if my writing had a deep relationship with this obfuscation and fear of conclusion. Despite my writing in these years mostly being now boarded up behind the ink of redaction, I still came out. I prevented myself from writing on those fears. Part of the reason for it was a rejection of something I now consider mostly mundane, but yes, part of it was out of a fear that someone might read it - someone far off in time whose own conclusions about my writing would shape who I was as a person. How narcissistic! I thought I was writing for myself, but I was writing these deeply personal and redacted texts for who I expected I should be, which was a   woman, again, despite all insistence through those blacked-out lines to the contrary.

I know better now - yes - much of the writing we do in notebooks trying to stay undistracted by a world begging so much for our attention as to see puerile will end up unread, reams of it were written under the expectation that they might, but maybe (hopefully, the sake of my younger fear) my handwriting isn't legible enough for this to happen, which I consider a blessing, because I was not narcissistic when I wrote them, and no one was supposed to read them, which is why I redacted the text I knew I needed to write, even though I policed my own language and even while I held back from ever becoming a writer because (among so many because) the idea that I was writing for no one in particular is merely graphomania. It's laughable, it makes sense, it doesn't.

Milan Kundera died on July 11th, 2023. I'm glad he is dead. I love his writing deeply, ignoring the fact of his searing lack of ability to properly process the concept of feminine identity as abstracted from the gaze of men, although I suppose there Chu agrees with him. I consider his writing to be of the greatest aesthetics, surpassing most of his contemporaries and my own. I think he was a weak ma, but not in the sense that men are pathetic for their weakness to vice as a tool for creativity, as Maggie Nelson sees them. Weak because I think his narcissism and his reflections upon it punctured the fabric of his identity as integral to all that he wrote. Many remember Nabokov for Pale Fire as a revelatory work with an unreliable narrator, Kundera was, and is, an unreliable author. I think Kundera did it best, I'm glad he's dead because I didn't understand what a person can do with a little narcissism and that little move cost me about nine to fifteen years depending on what we are talking about. Writing for me is or was an act of self-definition, both in the sense that what I have written defines me, but as well that what I chose not to write our of fear that someone might read it probably shaped just as much of the identity I now write from.

I'm resolved, in many ways, to write outside or through this graphomania. I wrote my notebooks fearful of who would read them. This was written in a notebook and will likely transform as I transcribe it (it did, I tell you now from the moment of transcription) into the format in which you read it. I am a narcissist. Who on earth would care what I have to say? Maybe you, because you've made it this far, you're already captive in the words I'm choosing, because I'm right in thinking someone would read them. I am a real writer, being poetic in the middle of my prose. You are a narcissist too, many of us are, we live in a domain and age of near continuous self-reflection-- it can all be documented because all of us believe someone will read it.

In a [2013 New Yorker article](https://archive.is/bimv5) by Thomas Beller that I haven't read because it is stuck neatly behind a paywall, Beller says that it was a "radical cri de Coeur to claim 'we live in public'" in the opening moves of the internet. Now this is mundane, I think. Some of us have elevated this form of expression across the strewn digitalia of the internet's walled gardens to a form of aesthetic sensibility that Kundera wasn't familiar with. I do believe this, though I have never been able to employ narcissism so concentrated into 280 characters or two minute videos as to afford me the feedback from my environment necessary to elevate my own self-interest out of graphomania. Not the collective one, my own, the notebooks I have filled and have never shared, that I wrote for people I am afraid will know me when I die. My narcissism requires more words.

Maybe this is why it is manic, if attention can only tolerate concentrated aesthetics, I'll never made it, I always hit the character count, but I am still trying to be a writer, for the first time. I'm not going to ask why, I'm going to be a little narcissistic and think that what I write is something I can improve on, that what I am writing could be interesting to ... Well, not everybody. But some of you, because I disagree with Kundera, which is why I'm glad he is dead. I'm also glad he's dead because he can't argue back, and I think he was too much a narcissist and a misogynist to have listened. I don't hate him. Every story for the unknown public has value. Mine does too. Banaka could have withheld his arrogance to learn how to teach. Kundera could have explained to his taxi driver that his mania could be steered toward aesthetics. He didn't. A million people on the Internet should into a 'public of unknown personas.' Thousands listen. It doesn't mean anything. We're all narcissists. It's beautiful. If I keep writing, maybe you'll read it.
