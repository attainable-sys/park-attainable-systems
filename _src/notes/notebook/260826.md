The Speed Project (TSP) is well-known in running culture, but not as much in the professional running scene. Those in the pro scene who do know about it fall under two camps - those who know it but think of it mostly as being silly, and those who understand that the popularity of running is not merely rooted in athleticism. This is not to say, necessarily, that TSP has in any way directed what we call running 'culture', but it certainly has done so in the past. Those who have done so across the years have been a tight collection of brands and race programs - a small crowd somehow penetrating beyond the fabric of locality into a globally-recognized phenomenon.
For my part, I've always thought that the current wave of 'running cool' owes a through-line to some of the first unsanctioned races that were popularized through Orchard Street Runners, with Joe DiNoto's Tuesday night runs out of New York City's Lower East Side having set the tone for the grit of 'urban running', whose aesthetic has reverberated over the last fourteen-or-so years. 2013 OSR was perfect for the Instagram era - grainy black-and-white images of athletes darting through traffic late at night - athletes covered in tattoos who gave off a recklessness built for admiration. At the time, the demographic of these runners skewed toward one group - the majority of those represented on social feed were white men putting themselves up against the risk factor of the concrete wilds. Joe, to massive credit, recognized this and worked to bring attention not only to the women who showed up, but as well the wider (non-white) breadth of runners who were willing to put themselves to the test. For a while, the critique of Joe's races was that while it liked to cast itself as 'urban running' - the permission of who got to call themselves 'urban runners' was open to debate; does urban mean white tattoed guy? Who else runs in these cities?
Again, to Joe's credit, he worked on changing this representation, any by the time of his final OSR Midnight Half in 2018, the field was as diverse as one might hope from a race that based itself in New York City.
For a while, OSR was in high demand from brands as a crucible for New York running culture. Adidas dangled the possibility of greater growth over everyone who contributed to OSR for years until Joe finally got fed up and shifted his attention to a bigger, more lenient brand in Nike, who continues to back his efforts today.
