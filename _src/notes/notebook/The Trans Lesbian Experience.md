When we find ourselves interrogating gender from the perspective of one who has gone through the experience of either binary identity in our society - I find it's almost impossible not to address the material reality of what a trans experience means in stark contrast to that of someone who grow up cis.

One of the cultural norms that I see time and time again are the encultured trans women ( usually in their first explorations of gender) polarizing themselves into the binary representation of what gender means to them, usually through the lens of stereotype. It makes for a startlingly binary and overall diminishing outward appearance to being trans - and it makes perfect sense, as Andrea Long Chu has pointed out, that trans women would look for representation in the form of objectifying pornography.

In much of feminization pornography (en brev: sissy porn), the tropes that are held up in representation are objectifying norms of gender - compliance, weakness, and most disturbingly, very often racially charged imagery. Being that a woman is allowed to be a sexual being, the trans woman who explores an identity of being sexual is prone to finding representation of themselves only in this context. The weaponized nature of this experience is one that is damaging to the utmost to someone who is in a fragile place while defining their expectations of gender. As a result, the sexual identity of a trans person is often framed through this lens, and the effects of this identity can be especially damaging - as trans lesbians will likely agree.

But more interesting is an underappreciated reality of experience with respect to gender, and it goes too often unrecognized. In particular here, I direct my attention to my experience - a trans lesbian, observing the acceptance of trans people into the fold of the broader lesbian experience. Jean Kilbourne argues herself that 'the self-esteem of girls plummets as they reach adolescence, partly because they cannot escape the message that their bodies are objects, and imperfect objects at that,' - and to put it bluntly, this is not the experience of a trans woman who passes through adolescence without addressing their transness as a part of identity-building, short of suppressing it. In having had the altogether less objectified experience of maleness in building ones adolescent identity, much of the time trans lesbians are able to avoid the trauma of degenerated identity that comes from having a world around you define you as a sexual object.

In identity building, trans women instead explore an identity of supremacy that they (usually) consciously reject - which is a reason that trans women (and trans people at large) do have well-founded understandings of gender dynamics in society. Many of the 'useful' traits of masculinity get held onto through utility - often because they are traits that all (cis and otherwise) women should have access to regardless. Too often, the manufacture of identity for trans women can become a careless navigation of conflating norms - oftentimes upholding a misogynistic view of womanhood learned through external experience in youth.

The danger to this is that a trans lesbian community can take part in toxic assumption - i.e. an unconscious equivalence between the cis and the trans experience of womanhood. Cis and trans women who go through a traditional experience of senescent adolescence don't have the same experience as all those women who grew up as cis men through that adolescence, meaning that a post-adolescent view of the experience of womanhood is necessarily going to be different between these experience.

I mention this largely and specifically due to a dangerous trend I see in online spaces. Where online spaces have been communicated widely be members of feminist writing as male-dominated spaces - the same can be said for the freedom of cis lesbians to create their own spaces online. The fearful reality of exploring ones own gender (as a trans person) and finding that they belong to an additionally marginalized group (lesbians) can often result in a competitive need for inclusion, and often muddies the safety of what the experience of a lesbian space is meant to provide.

In short, it seems pertinent to me that lesbian spaces concentrate on the experience of sexuality rather than the experience of gender - and while these two areas can intersect in terms of a sexual preference, conflating the experience of growing up cis with the experience of growing up trans can result in the erasure of commonality shared between people who grow up as cis lesbians. It's not the same experience. While I greatly appreciate the growth and acceptance of trans women into lesbian spaces, I fear the lack of caution that accompanies this integration because it does not beg a careful recognition of how intersectional experiences do indeed differ.

The cis lesbian experience is an experience of a cis person recognizing homosexual attraction. The trans lesbian experience is (more often than not) the experience of a cis-presenting person recognizing their transness and framing their initially heterosexually-defined attraction to women through the experience of their transness.
