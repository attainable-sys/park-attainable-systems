I wasn't going to write about this, but I think it probably makes more sense than it doesn't.

Before anything else, the UCI ruling does a ton of harm to a sport whose inclusivity I have seen evolve extensively over the last couple of decades. When I first entered athletics, I was hesitant for the exact reasons that the UCI is now choosing to rule around - I just don't want that much attention on me when I'm doing something I love.

Even though I'm not racing anymore (and I was never anyone significant when I was racing), this ruling confirms the feelings I'd been having around competition in the first place - dissuading people from participating in the ways that they can or should, and overall polluting the sport. What I could understand is a nuanced argument made around parties expressing a nuanced opinion, but that's not what we got. We got men (once again) expressing their opinions on what can and should be done with women's bodies.

I'm in running now, because running makes me feel free, because it makes me feel less anomalous - but as I've started to compete recently, I've started to wonder to myself ifthere's not a possibility that at some point they'll jump back and decide I can't compete anymore. Do I think I'm good at the sport? Sure - the reason for this is that I have worked for it. I train five days a week, I go to a personal trainer, I have a group of like-minded athletes who I compete alongside me and push me to be as good as I can me. This is the heart of sport, in the end, or at the least it's meant to be.

Competition is a chance to challenge yourself - less about competition against others and more about competition against oneself. The greatest athletes that I've known approached the sport this way - taking on the challenge for themselves first, with the results second. While there is competition between people in sport, part of the effort is to show some degree of sport[person]ship between parties, in order to encourage the notion that competition is not about measuring yourself against others.

I think that's why this ruling rubs me particularly wrong - the possibility of having to look as this as a question of 'fair competition' feels like an incomplete take on the question. Fair competition is a question moving through the field to evaluate from the position of those involved as to what is fair. I know any number of cis athletes who compete alongside their trans counterparts - and it's never them who stand and complain about athletic performance, because all of us understand the work that goes into being an athlete in the first place.

None of this was ever about trying to win. It was about competing alongside people we admire and being understood on the same level for the effort made. This being true, I don't understand why it's the place of a collective of men to make decisions about women's bodies, cis, trans, or otherwise. To categorize anyone's ability based on their experience is to reduce us to those experiences, and if anything, Caster Semenya's recent victory in the sport should be an indication that the experience and reality of gender are varied enough such that we shouldn't be asking these questions, we should be allowing people to be who they are.

For the love of the sport, I'd have expected that there was better measurement and adequate consideration given to people's lived experiences rather than any degree of armchair science that studies athletics. This is maybe where I have gotten the most frustrated - in an era of rationalism, we now like to turn to numbers to justify our decisions, instead of remembering that we are social creatures and that because of this there is probably more value in actually speaking to one another. Our experiences don't lie, which is probably why I feel, personally, so much more solidarity with the experiences of my cis counterparts. We share so much overlap and the further I move through my life of not being believed, not being listened to, not being attended to, and not being respected, the more I understand that we share these experiences because they are the same experiences.

If we were to be listened to, maybe this might be understood in the ways that would resolve situations like this. I can only speak for my experience, and I'd hope I would hear more from all those who have comparable experiences, from all those who I know share fragments of mine and want to share their own. Rationalism is a tool, not an ideological cudgel with which to make decisions; the decisions are meant to be separate from the information that we use to support them, we are meant to weigh data against the consensus of public opinion in order to come to our conclusions. That's not what is happening here, unfortunately.
