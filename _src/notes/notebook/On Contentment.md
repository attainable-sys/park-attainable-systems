Contentment can be such an odd companion to uncertainty.

I think most what has made me feel confused by contentment is particular to those moments where I feel as though I need to escape it - where everything in my life feels balanced and I have what I need, where I feel as though for some reason I can't quite get down to the math on what it is that I _want_.

It's not the first time that this sort of feeling has arisen, of course, there's a Restlessness in being a part of something foundational--a reaction to the relatively mundane nature of contentment that can feel like a requirement to escape it. I can't quite suss out what part of that process is something that I need to fear or something that I feel an imperative to respond to, because at the end of the day the real frustration I feel comes from the knowledge that I have everything I'm looking for and still seem to be looking for more. As such, is it dissatisfaction? Is the notion that one has found contentment a reaction to the principle of having to be 'happy'? What is the difference between contentment and happiness?

I'm not convinced that they are the same thing - contentment is a general sensation that reaches toward the mundane, while happiness is a punctuation within that emotion, a contentment as a baseline and a happiness as a peak that exists within the landscape that exists atop it.

And yet, contentment is something that we can revel in, it is the partner who comes home after a work day to make dinner with you, who sits on the couch to work while you paint in the adjoining room. Contentment _is_ neatly the mundane, but it is no less beautiful for this being true.

For if contentment is something that we have to react to toward the negative, it is just as much a sensation of not knowing what to do once we have what we are looking for, as though the taboo of having to adjust entirely to one's desire is a requirement. I'm not so certain that it is. When I break this down to an analysis of my own contentment, it's an experiment in assuming that I actually do know what I want.

I don't. This is the biggest challenge - I have a tendency to allow this to drift from me constantly, and because of this it's something that I feel I needed to more consciously explore because it keeps reacting to my presence and my ability to materialize the desires I have. The constant issue that comes with this is that materializing one's desires requires that we understand what those desires are motivated by, and to not have done the work to better understand what these are is specifically to doom oneself to the possibility of having to react, wholly, to whatever presents itself. That's not a way to live, because it never results in anything conscious.

I can't say that I necessarily understand my subconscious, either. I've been in conversation with it for years, attempting to understand what I value and what it is that I want to do with that estimation, but the wires that lead me toward my own systems of belief are somewhat out of reach for me conscious analysis without some sort of sincere contact witht hat subconscious. For a while I was doing this through the practice of therapy, but I've allowed that to take up mless presence in my life as I seek contentment - meaning that the general notion I'm forced to contend with is actually the realtively mundane existence that I experience in the presence of all the things that I've been looking for. I don't necessarily feel particularly pressed to have to evolve my conditions, if nothing else I need the reality of my convictions through the lens of my efforts, and I can see where it is that these things fall off.

The conversation with my subconscious notwithstanding, I can see that there's a notion of small sadness that accompanies seeing how I've let this life lead me rather than doing the reverse, the sentiment that I've been pushing myself to accommodate the reality that presents itself to me and to react to these opportunities as though they are something that I'm required to take on, but I can't make myself do that forever, because there is a distinct dissatisfaction with not doing what I know I want to be doing in the mean time, a fear that I've lost touch with what it is that I really care about.

Some of this can be traversed through consistent conversation with one or another therapeutic method or through sincere contemplation, but I only catch whispers of the goal. I hear things that tell me I care much more about things that have to do with moving my body and the stories that this inspires than I do with the requirement to embroil myself further into stories that need not expand themselves into the world, a notion of social imperative that is as risky as it is important--but I can't conceive of taking on this imperative without some due consideration for what it is that I actually want.

Most recently there has been the voice inside that says I 'want to be one of the greats' - fed by the notion that if one wants to be one of the greats, one must believe they deserve to be one of the greats. There is quantifiable and measured hubristic negligence in having to take on this notion, a necessity to push aside some partial fear that hubris will lead to downfall when in truth the consideration of one's own hubris can be placed in reflection to an environment that otherwise believes in our merit or at the least in our permission to believe in ourselves.

Belief is a part of this moral architecture that holds this imperative in place, where hubris can cause us to seek out that which might be costly and risky, belief is that which motivates us toward the otherwise illogical. To believe in oneself is to believe fully that the irrational parts of the self that make up the unconscious (depending, of course, on one's disposition) have some greater notion of self than the conscious parts that are forced to react to its motivations. A 'gut feeling' is a notion of understanding what we _know for sure_. We _only get a few things to really care about_, and it is our duty to respond when we better understand what it is that motivates us and what we need to do in order to see out a reaction to that inner truth.

A problem that is presented to use in this search, of course, is that the problem doesn't leap out at us from the page. We don't necessarily write and write and write each day expecting that we are going to come to some revelation, if anything this revelation comes from a steady and static reaction to the mundane that forces us to break patterns and rto realign ourselves with what matters. For instance, my understanding that there is something important in running could be logically discerned, but some of it is not logical, it is a part of the _logiciel_ that comes with being in the situation that make me feel the most congruence with my environment, a tendency toward respecting something much larger than me.

I like to believe that this is what I'm chasing, some abundant understanding of a plan that I'm fabricating in the back of my mind, but it's just a part of the execution, a fragment that I'm constantly seeking to outcompete with the logical (and verbose) parts of who I am. The dialogue between me and that subroutine is constant, and what I'm forced to do out of respect for the less verbal and far more emotional parts of myself is to allow its reactions to carry the same weight as the logical conclusions I am capable of interpreting through the tried method of continuous self-application. Discernment is a process by which we take the information in front of us and attempt to better understand the whole - a blind mouse doing it's best to visualize the elephant.

Writing this of course does not bring some sort of grand conclusion to my eyes or mind, it's more of the same, a traversal of one's own continuous reaction to the Truth of existence. So while it's easy to be disappointed, it's equally important to ask oneself within the realm of possibility whether or not the opportunities that we are presented with are things that we know for sure we want to react to. Consciousness is a discernment of reality. Unconsciousness is our experience of it. A rationalist view of reality is to misunderstand that we 'create' reality only within the confined of that which we seek to interpret, but reality does not care about us, and that's okay.

While the question continues to orbit; what do I really want, what do I want it to look like, the reality of the notion refuses to fall short of answering the question and is almost certain to result in the conclusions to which we feel most aligned so long as we are respectful of our unconscious. This is not to say that we are required to take on opportunity, but rather that our reactions to opportunity require a sort of discernment that acknowledges that which we are capable of and that which we truly desire, and reconciles these two in order to find that which offers us the best conditions for both contentment and joy.

All the while I understand the importance of hardship, even of uncertainty, and the edge conditions that unfold from a requirement to exist at the edges of disturbance - Tsing's notion of [[salvage]] is what most speaks to this reality-breaking form of existence, an exploitation of an extant imbalance that allows us to exist in a state of constant adaptation that brings with it its own form of liminal joy. But this is an idealization because hardship as a requirement of joy is a self-defeating construct that we can use to force one another into conditions of negligence and disuse - and we as a species and as a consciousness deserve more than this.

It then becomes important to question whether or not in the more content periods it is more or less likely that we can believe in the [[adjacent possible]] - as a product of our desire to depart austere conditions we dream of better outcomes for ourselves, and this often becomes the motivator for assuring the [[action potential]] accrues sufficiently so to overcome conditions and strike out for more and better in oneself. I'm not convinced, however, that it is a requirement, because I believe that some of the greatest minds lived otherwise mundane existences and it was (is) the turmoil they experienced within themselves at a disagreement with reality over its basic functions that produced the conditions of [[salvage]] inspiring an exploration of the adjacent possible. Our ability to formulate turmoil within ourselves is thus a gift, a tool that we can use to motivate ourselves in exploring new [[local minima]] in the [[punctuated equilibrium]] of how we comprehend existence on both conscious and unconscious levels.

Creating conditions for contentment then maybe affords us the ability to think at grander scales abject of our experience on the local scale - if our needs are met, we are able to think outside of ourselves and evaluate problems that exist on a [[superorganismal]] scale. This being the case, contentment and the punctuated joy of existing are not somehow at a disagreement with the idea of progress - if anything they are foundational for thinking at larger scales, because the scope of one's [[area of effect]] is a product of how much influence someone has over their surroundings not through power, but through the collaborative tendency to participate in a collective reasoning that supercedes the [[self-interested individual]].

A requirement of existing at scale and thriving withing one's 'potential' is then to bring the collective mission to a local stasis in as many environments as possible while combatting the likelihood of this meaning that self-same hubris results in conditions that decrease the degrees of opportunity available to others. If the tendency of the universe is to expand, this means that degrees of latitude are growing at a constant rate, the same can be both true and tried for.

The conclusion is then that we can materialize conditions of local [[contentment]] for ourselves, and that they can be foundational for us to seek out new and higher states. A state of contentment is a local minimus from which we are able to seek out the adjacent possible, and it's not a requirement to suffer in order to push oneself into such a state, because we are capable of considering a collective condition as equally important to that of our experience at the local scale. It means that we as individuals can take on an imperative to stabilize our condition in order to encourage the stabilization of others - and that this offers meaningful conditions within which we can continue to work collectively toward the betterment and growth of the human project. I remain somewhat convinced of this--held loosely, of course, as with any belief--but I believe fully in the importance of such a notion, because thinking this way seems to create the best conditions under which what is possible becomes less negligible, and minimizes reactionary belief that seeks only to escape locally detrimental conditions.
