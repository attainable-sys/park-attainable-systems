The real question, I'd suppose, is whether or not the life I have lived has really gotten in the way of who I am. By most stretches, I've had a very accomplished career - there are not many people who can say that they worked with National Geographic and held a flash bulb over Tutankhamun's forehead, there are not many who can say that they became the CEO of a tech project only 6 years later, or that whilst doing either of these things I ran several marathons and eventually several ultramarathons. My most stretches, I can respect the assumption that I've been able to make do with the experience that I've had, and that I'm doing just fine.

However, the issue is not whether or not I've been able to achieve things - it's that I've been able to accomplish these achievements despite what I would consider to have been fairly significant hurdles in the large scope of things, and possibly even because of them. I'd make the argument that the reason I'm able to pull off the massive efforts that I have in the past is because I have more experience putting up with a bad experience than most people do, so when the time comes to choose some or other difficult experience, I'm already prepared by my experience with fear, disappointment, trial, and exhaustion to endure whatever amount of effort it takes to accomplish something. It does not devalue the achievement, in my regard, to acknowledge this; I am proud of the efforts I have made and what it has taken for me to achieve them, and I will continue to take them on. In large part, they are the reason you are reading this body of text which otherwise I probably wouldn't have written due to a mild shame I feel in recognizing the exhaustion I have felt, time and again, so soon as stopping for a moment to understand the path charted toward the present.

Partially, the reason for my inclination to beat around the bush with 'how hard it has been to be me' is because of what I've been able to accomplish, and since what I've been able to accomplish has been substantially more than that most people can lay claim to, I usually push the hurdles and challenges to the side as though they aren't quite deserving of the attention this letter demands.

However, if there hurdles to be acknowledged, there are three: I moved 9 times across 8 countries before I was 16; I have diagnosed, unmedicated, yet well managed ADHD; I am of mixed latina ethnicity; I am a transgendered woman.

I'd pause here for a moment and recommend that you ask yourself, before reading any further, if any of these three call up biases in you. I could understand it, especially given the current stretch of time in which you have read those words, maybe more for the latter two than the first - identity politics has run rampant in our society to such a degree as to devalue the impact of these experiences into a normalizing minimization - but I feel these experiences frame the brunt of why it is I have taken until the age of 35 to follow the academic career I'm now seeking to pursue. Further, I lament that they are the case, because under the ideological regime with which we now examine people, I find that utilizing words like 'transgender woman' and 'ADHD' are highly reductive on the basis of the exact biases I am asking you, the reader, to interrogate.

The primary affliction of being a third-culture kid is probably less commonly understood, as is being of mixed ethnicity, so they deserve priority - particularly due to their relationship with the latter experiences and how they have framed my career so far, academic or otherwise. I've lived in the Netherlands, France, Canada, Connecticut, Dubai, Luxembourg (two different homes), Italy, and finally Georgia, where I graduated high school. What this meant, for me, was that I moved between 8 different school systems before finally graduating high school in the last county in the United States to desegregate (Forsyth County did not have a significant black population until 1987, and when I graduated in 2008, my AP physics course was taught out of the answer book chained to the wall at the back of the classroom).

It meant that by the time I graduated high school, the particular frustration I had to endure was the lack of compatibility between international systems of academic evaluation for mathematics that resulted in, on one hand, my taking algebra and trigonometry three different times in three different countries, and on the other hand, a general apathy for continuing on with mathematics. It meant then when I got to university and had almost forgotten the time I'd spent huddled across a table with my primary school friends, excitedly competing with one another to get to the end of our algebra problems. It meant that overall, my parents hadn't thought that I should prioritize this sort of thinking - so I didn't. I went to college, I got a degree that I am proud of, but that didn't require substantial quantitative reasoning beyond statistics. I regret this.

University presented me with another difficulty - throughout much of my adolescence I had kept a secret. Queerness prior to the 2010s wasn't publicly spoken on in meaningful ways outside of a fight for acceptance, and trans people especially were not spoken about with any degree of respect; the secret I had been keeping, and that I would continue to keep until I was 27, was that while raised as a man, I am a woman.

In university, I tried to address this a few times - I spoke to counsellors in free sessions offered by the school to see if it was something that was real, that it wouldn't be dismissed out of hand. Being that I went to school in the state of Georgia, I wasn't given any meaningful support there, and I buried it deeper. This exacerbated another part of my identity that I've had to struggle with, in the form of my ADHD.

ADHD is an anxiety disorder - as much as people understand it as an issue of attention (it turns out that ADHD is not a lack of attention, but rather an abundance of it), it is usually attention that flickers between subjects on the basis of an underlying nervous tendency. Keeping my gender identity under wraps during my college years meant that while I was able to get through college, it was against a tide of anxious repression that often deeply impacted my schoolwork, meaning that the only good semesters I had were those where I was able to grit my teeth and ignore the storming unrest inside of me to make progress on my grades. This never fixed the depression that comes with repression, and usually the depression won, more semesters than not.

The unfortunate result of this is that by the time I graduated school, the parts of me that needed the most attention weren't given what they asked for, and what I was supposed to be doing in the mean time suffered for it. I continued on with repressing my identity for another 5 years after graduation, struggling through the impact it had on my ability to pursue more complex work, and I succeeded despite it; I had a burgeoning photography career, taking me to incredible places that I was probably only safe to visit with people perceiving me as a man. I don't regret these experiences, they are some of the most memorable I've ever had - but I know that my safety was protected in large part by the gender identity that I was showing the world at the time.

When I turned 27, I had just terminated a relationship with someone I had tried to come out to, and I was ready, finally, to address what was going on within me. I didn't want to continue shouldering the burden of the effect it had on who I was - and the act of telling the truth to myself and then beginning to tell it to everyone else opened me up to everything else about myself that I could address. I was also fortunate - in 2017 people were truly beginning to speak about transgender people as though we should be recognized more legitimately, and with this came a comfort that coming out would bring me closer to myself, and that my identity was legitimate without being some sort of idealization of gender.

It is remarkable for me to look back on the effect that coming out had on me. I pivoted entirely within two years of coming out: I stopped being in photography, I worked very hard on understanding my ADHD and how to address it, and I pivoted to a quantitative career. I look back on the history that got me here and I acknowledge that I had a very interesting successful career despite my burdens, but that I am now doing what I actually care about. I am incredibly proud of this progress, because it isn't something that many have the privilege (and honestly, the stubbornness) on which to hold fast. I have now been a female CEO, which in itself in an achievement, and the brunt of the reason for this was because I have had the experience that brought me into the present, a knowledge of a patriarchal world that disadvantages those it is not made for. Learning from my burdens made me better at what I do.

I think this last point bears repeating: I am proud of who I am, but I also recognize that everything I've had to deal with,--either isolated to my experiences of life in having moved so many times, in what society has only recently become comfortable with accepting-- is still a challenge. It's made worse today by an ideological regime that seems to be resistant to this sort of flexibility and acceptance, but we know why this is: when new truths are made evident, it is always the case that the dominant power structure will resist change before it is made inevitable. I have won against odds and with the support of a family, friends, and a society that is coming to accept people with my experience, and I am glad for having it, but I also know it is a major factor in what has prevented me from pursuing who I now aim to be.
