Five days across the desert and I sat huddled close to a fire that fought the same wind I did with my back. I had tied my horse to what remained of a tree, the skeleton something once living, a shape that arched its tension against the elements but even whose roots threatened to curl upward, but I tied my horse just the same, and my horse stood, and I gave it tack and it stood ready for the night, I unloaded the satchels and the saddle and rolled out the blanket, broke off some of those dry roots from a long dead tree to start a flame that was destined to fight the same wind I was bound to. I could not call it a fire, it was little more than light, and the moonless sky above was littered with the pebbles of light cast out to remind me that I was sinking into an abyss, reminding me that I did not have a reason to be in this forsaken desert for what  I had lost before me was nothing that enriched and where I was going was too unknown to offer me solace in my wandering.

I sat across my shivering little wick and somewhere in the hours that passed staring quiet into the flame, in the dark I saw a shape approach in the dark. Quick as the wind, I drew my revolver and pointed it at the dark and its hands were thrown up, and a cowboy approached, dried gaunt by the expanse just as was I. He stood there a while, then rasped like the ache of old sand not moved for a summer, he asked to sit by the fire. I kept my barrel trained on him, he said he'd he'd share wood and whiskey. I let him sit, I lowered the barrel but kept it in hand, and kept my eyes sharp below my had at the revolver handing off his left hip. He sat and tossed his flask to me into the sand at my feet, unrolled his blanket, stared into the flame, then rolled and lit a cigarette from his lighter, rolled another, tossed it my way, my hand still on my gun. He said he'd not stay beyond my rest, that he would keep moving, I already knew this meant I'd have to do the same, that I would not sleep this night, but he sat and stared into the flame and neither of us said anything for a long time.

Finally, after having pulled off a third cigarette, he spoke, eyes looking past the flames.
"Do you know the story of the Minotaur?"
The question hang for a moment in the air between us, crackling with the twigs that lit the night. My horse had lain down to my right, finally some shielding from the wind. I said I had not.
”Well - There is the story of Theseus and the labyrinth, and there is the Minotaur. The story goes that Minos fed the Minotaur 14 virgins every nine years, because to preserve the abomination as an extension of the self is to understand that an abomination cannot be eliminated; there is purpose in the abomination as an evidence of the self - or put more plainly: Minos kept the Minotaur alive because there was something about it that reminded him of himself."

He leant forward, ashing his cigarette once again into the wisps of flame between us, the tip of the tobacco glowing a cinder below the brim of his hat as he pulled another drags between threats. The glow lit his eyes, and he was not looking at me.

"But you don't know the rest of the story. The story goes that Minos had a beautiful daughter in his kingdom on the island of Crete, and that her beauty was as much a legend at that of the Minotaur itself. The Minotaur was kept alive by his daughter's will, and the maze they kept it in was merely to keep it occupied as it charged in a rage through the halls of the labyrinth. Minos contracted Daedalus to build the labyrinth as a response and a tribute to his daughter’s fascination and fear of the unknown, a fear of how much she would never know, and a desire to make real the needs of his daughter who he did not understand and who he himself feared for what she did.
I looked out at the flow of his cigarette underneath the brim of his hat, peeling from the sun.
"Ariadne as a young woman was beautiful beyond reproach and this is a truth as well told of the story that most know--it is this which attracted Theseus to Crete--but what is lost from the legend as well is her intellect and the knowledge that she scoured from the earth; the studies she demanded her father offer even as a young child, indeed Daedalus was not brought to Crete only to be an inventor for Minos, but as well her mentor and teacher, and the more she read, the more she feared the unknown, until finally she did find this unknown so unspeakably large that she could not sustain the fear. Before he found her along the deadly heights of his palace, she had begun to speak of her fear that meaning cannot be found among the pages she read, nor in any comfort that Daedalus attempted to offer for the pleasure of the search, nor in any of the minds of men--dead or a live--who had spoken on how to seek it. Hell.
He spat into the flames, picked at his teeth, then went on.
"She began to fear the unknown and that which lie within it, the unimagined and the fearsome truths that subtend a fragile sense of existing among what is barely understood, beasts made from these shadows that cannot be explained. Minos, nor Daedalus, nor anyone, could offer her solace, and she plunged deeper and deeper into these multiplying fears, and began to claim she could see them, these beasts of horns and matted fur that trudge through the night, abominations born of her discoveries as she plunged further into knowledge so deeply uncommon to any who surrounded her. In time, she began to claim that beasts came to her at night and when she wandered the palace grounds, they chased her, and she would come to her father wild-eyed, drenched in the fear of pursuit.

Minos doubled the guard upon these claims, lighting raging fires to illuminate the palace, and he asked his daughter not to steal away on her own lest these beasts (for these beasts did also exist in the stories of others in their time) come claim his daughter. It was not until that fateful night with the wind rushing up the battlements he sprang forth to catch her before she passed over them , speaking of a beast that was half-bull and half-man, a beast that she knew for certain  was borne of her mother when she had died and who was borne of Zeus, not her father--and her father's fear was confirmed that she knew and remembered how her mother had died in the attempt to bring him an heir , but that as well she had die d whilst he had been away in battle.

He began to think it possible that his son may have been born an abomination and that it was because of this that he had never seen the young boy's body when he had returned, that they had loosed the abomination, and that the fear that Ariadne claimed was true, for she was wise for her years and loved him and he loved her and this trust was no reason for a deception. He began to believe that his son was born of Zeus and that he had grown into a fearsome beast that pursued his daughter just as she claimed, and even while he continued to increase the guard, his daughter's fears never lessened.

The real reason that Minos built the labyrinth is because as the Minotaur began to chase his daughter even in her dreams, he once found her at the edge of Knossos, naked and staring into a gale that tore up the battlements, and he feared the resolution he saw in her eyes that defied the solace he had seen in them so many times before, her desire for oblivion to be found dashed among the rocks below. Never again was she allowed to be alone,"

The cowboy then went silent for a while, and the stillness of our shared flame flickered to beckon him, as though the desert itself were listening to the gravel of his voice. He rolled another cigarette, tossed it my way, then rolled one more with what was left of his tobacco.

"For many years she lived under this constant guard, and she began to become bitter, she began to spit hateful venom toward her father for keeping her so entrapped, but he feared what he had seen in her that fateful night and he knew that it was only through his authority that his daughter would remain with him.

One morning she came to him, on a day filled with shadow, she had awoken late, she spoke, far more calm than she had been in months, now a young woman who had grown dark and beautiful, and she looked into his eyes with a love that he had not seen since she was a child, but with a wildness that he had no choice but to answer, and she looked upon him with wonder and wildness in her eyes: *I know now how to capture the Minotaur that troubles my sleep. I know how to capture my brother, and I know how to trap him forever.*

She spoke to her father of this massive fearful beast, and that as many nights she was trapped in the battlements of the palace, he had come to her and stood fearsome in her bedroom, a mighty shape of contoured muscle, shoulders that were impossibly large, horns that curled and scraped against the ceiling of her room, heavy, rage-filled eyes that had stared into her as a demons do, eyes that fixed her to her bed. She claimed that the beast approached her bed and stood at the end, and that she could smell the want in its breath, indeed she could see its want, extended and rigid before her, and that it had done this many nights. In her terror she had sought for years to understand how to stop it from arriving, and she said she knew in those eyes how to trap it, but that it must be in a maze, and that only she could trap it.
"She said to her father through the glint and wonder that he understood to be a fire of inspiration that he must build a labyrinth, that Daedalus must build it for her, and that she alone would attract the beast to her in the halls of the maze. Minos of course looked at her with fear and the anxiety of hopelessness and saw in those same glittering eyes the certainty hard set that he must follow, that he could not deny this request.
"And so the labyrinth was built, with walls and tunnels, over the course of three long years. In this time his daughter seemed to be subdued in her fear, always learning from Daedalus and in consort with his son, Icarus, whose own legend you also may know. The limestone of its walls was milled and mined from the mountains far to the east, and it cost Minos much of his fortune, his authority holding on in the pursuit of this magnanimous challenge only as a way to ensure that the lands were under his control. He was fortunate in the relative peace of the time, and his armies were put to work in construction, unquestioning, well fed, and all in doubt of the Minotaur's presence.
"You see in that time, it may well have been that these lands were under the authority of Minos, but it weren't the case that people believed the legend of the Beast: no one seen it, but then again maybe beasts like this one didn't come to the common folk. Minos of course was purported to be ordained with the divine right of being a ruler, under the authority of the gods, with his seers (whether truthful or not, of course) conferring with these gods to speak on this very same right.
"Things got a little shakier when the labyrinth was done, however, because this fella had the gaul to then tell everyone out in his little kingdom that there needed to be a sacrifice made to the Minotaur. No one knew where this came from - remember now that no one had actually seen the thing, so there was a lot to the story to tell. Even in those days some king stepping up to say that you had to kill a bunch of kids to appease the gods was met with unrest, and since no one wanted their kids to be the ones picked, Minos had to launch a campaign of war to take kids from the farmers of the mountains for this purpose, avoiding this very unrest.
“Now, you may have been told that Minos fed his labyrinth these fourteen virgins every seven years, but this is an apocryphal note - it ain't the whole story. It was a night without a moon where his daughter told him she'd was going to sleep at the mouth of the maze, in that first good hall of many turns that she had Daedalus build up with one-way doors to trap the beast inside. She knew the Minotaur would come to find her, following her scent around the winding turns, and she would vanish so soon as she saw it.
"That night that she slept in the labyrinth Minos didn't get any sleep of course, worried sick about her, and he half thought he should abandon the whole things - he got his whole army stirred up thinking they might have to kill the thing as it came to the door - there was only one way in that complex of tall, winding passageways, so that meant there was only one way for the Minotaur to arrive. Of course Ariadne saw this and got all up in her own anger - saying to her father that she couldn't understand how the hell he'd defy a god, knowing that it was a child of Zeus and her mother - that it was not up to him whether or not it died, and it was not up to him if even he saw the beast.
"Minos of course didn't know what to do here other than back his troops down from their readiness. He was middle-aged then, his daughter solidly a woman who had done nothing but live her life in the prison her dad had built up around her out of fear she'd ever disappear. At this point he didn't really have a way to say he could have done otherwise. So that night, she slept in the labyrinth, and maybe the Minotaur came in, no one really knew because no one really knew if it was real at all. It was the next day that really twisted everyone's ears over what his daughter commanded him to do, being the only one to say that the Beast had been captured.
"Truth is, the legend varies down to every year, added to the story in the years since it was told for the first time. It is, anyway, in the nature of the truth that is being told - virgins were fed into the labyrinth, but it was out of respect for the madness that existed within, the madness that the labyrinth represented, the madness that the Minotaur embodied, and that this madness is not one of a beast, but one that is present in all of man.
"There were a good few people in the kingdom who got to figuring out that this was probably just some madness taken on by the king. All the people in his land knew he loved his daughter. Truth on the story is that she was the one who told him that he had to feed the virgins to the maze. She said that if that beast really was a child of Zeus and not his own, then it could not be killed by any common man, and must be kept alive. But Minos loved that daughter more than he did anything else in that land, so the kids were sacrificed hook or crook into the labyrinth, that complex built over an orchard and lands filled with springs: if the kids survived, they might a while on their own in its halls.
"What is always left out is that in all those years that these kids were fed into the labyrinth, their bodies were never found, that those two trailing sacrifices of those groups of kids into the maze every good few years were done with the expectation that the Minotaur remains within the labyrinth, that they would be gored and consumed, but none of this is reliable as you know; there is no Minotaur within the labyrinth, at least so far as people could tell. Most of the time, it seemed like Minos' mad daughter called all the shots on what needed to happen, and it was never really a question one way or another whether or not her daddy's armies would have to go out and steal some poor family's kids out from under them to loose into the complex built up just down the hill from his palace. Some people did fight back, a'course, but no real way of having townfolk get together up in the mountains to put up any sort of fight against what his armies were commanded to do.
"However, in all those years that Minos ruled over the land, he hadn't forgotten what his wild-eyed daughter had said about how that beast could be kilt. Remember now that she'd said in all that bluster that it wasn't up to him if the beast died, that only a great hero on par with the gods would have the right to take it down.
"So he put a call out across the lands for great heroes to come and kill this beast once and for all so as to free his daughter, and he promised his daughter's hand and partial rule over his kingdom were anyone to come in and succeed it killing the Minotaur.
"No one really answered that call for a good decade or more because he'd meet with some of these kids who'd send him letters all full of the decision that they'd kill the monster, but it never really seemed like many of them were up to it.
"That is, until stories of Theseus and his conquests arrived at the king's tired ears.
For a long moment, the dark figure across from me didn't stir. Finally, he uncorked the mouth of the handle of whiskey, brought it to his hidden lips and drained what was left. He then reached for a saddlebag at his side, pulled out another handle, opened a skin of water, took another long draw into the shadow under the brim of his hat, and settled back against the saddle he lay up against.
The wind had subsided now, and the cold started to seep in as it often does in the desert, the stars reeling overhead. I put more of the broken roots and dried cruft left of the sad tree we sat beside into the embers, coaxing it back into flame. The stranger said nothing, but the heat bloomed up from the pit, and I sat back down, hoping he would continue. He finally stirred, threw the handle my way, and continued:
"When Theseus arrived on the shores of Crete and burst into the halls of Knossos, he was a loud son, and that volume was offensive - many such heroes exist in the stories of Greece, and all of them have this same flavor once they've got a few victories under their belt. There is no passion like the bluster of believing that you can overcome what the world presents to you. He burst into the hall to face Minos as a king, and in Theseus Minos found a chance to raise his daughter from the terror he saw in her, thinking he finally had found a way to rid his daughter of her burden.
"And so when he introduced Theseus to his daughter, he did so with motive. Ariadne had eyes like the setting sun in winter, lost, aching for a place that didn't exist. Her hair was a wild labyrinth of its own, bright red, and she lined her eyes with the ashes of the fires she used to light pages and pages of books, deep into the night, so that her eyes had a sullen quality.
"When she entered the main hall at her father’s calling, Theseus was driven wild at her eyes and her hair and called to her that he had come to slay the Minotaur and her laugh sounded to him like the twinkling of glass, which he mistook as fragility. Minos saw this and knew that it was good and felt comfort, some hope that this confident young man would bring her out of the depths. His love for her was a desperation, because he didn't know how he could save from her own fear his daughter who he kept trapped at Knossos.
"His daughter, on the other hand, had other plans. You oughta remember that at this point she'd been trapped in Knossos her whole life, and a person that smart ain't anything but angry. When Theseus met Ariadne she knew exactly who he was and saw through him in knowing this; she saw his confidence and his willingness and she felt the dark pool of Night that lived within her stir, so she listened and cracked a smile that was every bit that which had pulled Theseus across the ocean after the will of conquest: a smile of an open snowy expanse yawning siren-like when the cold is too much. Theseus followed the flame in his own eyes and was in love, and she knew this, like a lion knows its prey can't escape.
The embers crackled, I hugged my blanket in closer, and the moon's sliver cast no light upon us. The cowboy continued .
"Theseus stayed in the halls of Knossos for a month preparing for the expedition into the labyrinth, requesting of her father all manner or supply. Ariadne in this time seemed bright, brighter than her father had ever seen her. It gave him hope to see that maybe this man was the man who would save her - and she hadn't seen many eligible men her age in all those years 'cept the guards and Icarus, but this was after Daedalus and Icarus had made their escape and the young boy's body had washed ashore his kingdom just after they tried.
"Ariadne on the other hand was a lot more of a thinker than her dad could fathom to expect at this point. She was willing to help the hero who had to come to save her, spending long hours speaking with him in the hearth of the palace, late into the night, listening quietly when he felt the need to tell her of his stories. She sat, listened, sometimes said one nice thing or other. Theseus was of course so taken with her he didn't see those signs maybe the gears were turning up in that quiet head of hers. She was older than him by a shot. At this point she'd had a long time to think.
So the night before they'd all agreed he was going to go to the labyrinth, she sent for him to follow her to her chambers and in those chambers she gave him a golden thread, the same one you've heard of before in this story. She told him that he'd only be able to enter that maze once, but that this thread would help make sure he wouldn't get lost.

"He looked upon her with her eyes that plumbed his depths and he believed her, that if only to conquer the minotaur would she be conquered for himself"

----

For to Ariadne the labyrinth was to sacrifice souls into the story of exploration so as to make a point of what it means to seek too much of the unknown. It was this fearful knowledge that she held within herself to which she held a flame of her reading fires each night; that she need avoid some corners of her own thoughts for fear they would swallow her as the labyrinth would swallow those she commanded her father to sacrifice into it, and their disappearance was confirmation if nothing else, that you should not explore that which you are not equipped to understand.
