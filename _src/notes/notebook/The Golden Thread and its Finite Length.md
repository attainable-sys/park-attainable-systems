Five days across the desert and I sat huddled close to a fire that fought the same wind I did with my back. I had tied my horse to what remained of a tree, the skeleton something once living, a shape that arched its tension against the elements but even whose roots threatened to curl upward, but I tied my horse just the same, and my horse stood, and I gave it tack and it stood ready for the night, I unloaded the satchels and rolled out the blanket, broke off some of those dry roots to start a flame that was destined to fight the same wind I was bound to. I could not call it a fire, it was little more than light, and the moonless sky above was littered with the pebbles of light cast out to remind me that I was sinking into an abyss, reminding me that I did not have a reason to be in this forsaken desert for what  I had lost before me was nothing that enriched and where I was going was too unknown to offer me solace in my wandering.

I sat across my shivering little wick and in the dark I saw a shape approach in the dark, I drew my revolver and pointed it at the dark and its hands were thrown up, and a cowboy approached, dried gaunt by the expanse just as was I, and he asked to sit by the fire, I kept my barrel trained on him, he said he'd he'd share wood and whiskey, I let him sit, I lowered the barrel but kept it in hand. He sat and tossed his flask to me, he unrolled his blanket, stared into the flame, then rolled and lit a cigarette from his lighter, rolled another, tossed it my way, my hand still on my gun. He said he'd not stay beyond my rest, that he would keep moving, I already knew this meant I'd have to do the same, that I would not sleep this night, but he sat and stared into the flame and neither of us said anything for a long time.

Finally, after having pulled off a third cigarette, he spoke, eyes looking past the flames.

”There is the story of Theseus and the labyrinth, and there is the Minotaur. Minos fed the Minotaur 14 virgins every nine years, because to preserve the abomination as an extension of the self is to understand that an abomination cannot be eliminated; there is purpose in the abomination as an evidence of the self.”

He leant forward, ashing his cigarette once again into the wisps of flame between us, the tip of the tobacco glowing a cinder below the brim of his hat as he pulled another drags between threats. The glow lit his eyes, and he was not looking at me.

"Minos contracted Daedalus to build the labyrinth as a response and a tribute to his daughter’s fascination and fear of the unknown, a fear of how much she would never know, and a desire to make real the needs of his daughter who he did not understand and who he himself feared for what she did.

Ariadne as a young woman was beautiful beyond reproach and this is a truth as well told of the story that most know--it is this which attracted Theseus to Crete--but what is lost from the legend as well is her intellect and the knowledge that she scoured from the earth; the studies she demanded her father offer even as a young child, indeed Daedalus was not brought to Crete only to be an inventor for Minos, but as well her mentor and teacher, and the more she read, the more she feared the unknown, until finally she did find this unknown so unspeakably large that she could not sustain the fear. Before he found her along the deadly heights of his palace, she had begun to speak of her fear that meaning cannot be found among the pages she read, nor in any comfort that Daedalus attempted to offer for the pleasure of the search, nor in any of the minds of men--dead or a live--who had spoken on how to seek it.

She began to fear the unknown and that which lie within it, the unimagined and the fearsome truths that subtend a fragile sense of existing among what is barely understood, beasts made from these shadows that cannot be explained. Minos, nor Daedalus, nor anyone, could offer her solace, and she plunged deeper and deeper into these multiplying fears, and began to claim she could see them, these beasts of horns and matted fur that trudge through the night, abominations born of her discoveries as she plunged further into knowledge so deeply uncommon to any who surrounded her. In time, she began to claim that beasts came to her at night and when she wandered the palace grounds, they chased her, and she would come to her father wild-eyed, drenched in the fear of pursuit.

Minos doubled the guard upon these claims, lighting raging fires to illuminate the palace, and he asked his daughter not to steal away on her own lest these beasts (for these beasts did also exist in the stories of others in their time) come claim his daughter. It was not until that fateful night with the wind rushing up the battlements he sprang forth to catch her before she passed over them , speaking of a beast that was half-bull and half-man, a beast that she knew for certain  was borne of her mother when she had died and who was borne of Zeus, not her father--and her father's fear was confirmed that she knew and remembered how her mother had died in the attempt to bring him an heir , but that as well she had die d whilst he had been away in battle.

He began to think it possible that his son may have been born an abomination and that it was because of this that he had never seen the young boy's body when he had returned, that they had loosed the abomination, and that the fear that Ariadne claimed was true, for she was wise for her years and loved him and he loved her and this trust was no reason for a deception. He began to believe that his son was born of Zeus and that he had grown into a fearsome beast that pursued his daughter just as she claimed, and even while he continued to increase the guard, his daughter's fears never lessened.

The real reason that Minos built the labyrinth is because as the Minotaur began to chase his daughter even in her dreams, he once found her at the edge of Knossos, naked and staring into a gale that tore up the battlements, and he feared the resolution he saw in her eyes that defied the solace he had seen in them so many times before, her desire for oblivion to be found dashed among the rocks below. Never again was she allowed to be alone,"

The cowboy then went silent for a while, and the stillness of our shared flame flickered to beckon him, as though the desert itself were listening to the gravel of his voice. He rolled another cigarette, tossed it my way, then rolled one more with what was left of his tobacco.

"For many years she lived under this constant guard, and she began to become bitter, she began to spit hateful venom toward her father for keeping her so entrapped, but he feared what he had seen in her that fateful night and he knew that it was only through his authority that his daughter would remain with him.

But one day she came to him, far more calm than she had been in months, now a young woman who had grown dark and beautiful, and she looked into his eyes with a love that he had not seen since she was a child, but with a wildness that he had no choice but to answer, and she said 'I know how to capture the Minotaur. I know how to capture your son so that he no longer pursues me, and I know that he will be trapped forever'

She spoke to her father of this massive fearful beast, and that before she was trapped in the battlements he had come to her and stood fearsome in her bedroom, a mighty shape of contoured muscle, shoulders that were impossibly large, horns that curled and scraped against the ceiling of her room, heavy, rage-filled eyes that had stared into her as a demons do, eyes that fixed her to her bed. She claimed that the beast approached her bed and stood at the end, and that she could smell the want in its breath, indeed she could see its want, extended and rigid before her, and that it had done this many nights. In her terror she had sought for years to understand how to stop it from arriving, and she said she knew in those eyes how to trap it, but that it must be in a maze, and that only she could trap it.

	“You may have been told that Minos fed his labyrinth these fourteen virgins every seven years, but this is an apocryphal note. Truth is, the legend varies down to every year, added to the story in the years since it was told for the first time. It is, nonetheless, in the nature of the truth that is being told - virgins were fed into the labyrinth, but it was out of respect for the madness that existed within, the madness that the labyrinth represented, the madness that the Minotaur embodied, and that this madness is not one of a beast, but one that is present in all of man."

THAT'S IT SO FAR

What is always left out is that the bodies of the virgins are never found, that the two trailing sacrifices to Zeus were done with the expectation that the Minotaur remains within the labyrinth, that they would be gored and consumed, but the incongruence of this is reliable; there is no Minotaur within the labyrinth.

When Theseus arrived on the shores of Crete and burst into the halls of Knossos, he was boisterous, and his volume was offensive - many such heroes exist in the stories of Greece, and all of them have this same flavor, there is no passion like that of believing that you can overcome that which the world presents to you. He burst into the hall to face Minos as a king, and in Theseus Minos found a chance to raise his daughter from the terror he saw in her, the fear he had of what lay within her.

And so when he introduced Theseus to his daughter, he did so with motive. Ariadne had eyes like the setting sun in winter, lost, aching for a place that does not exist. Her hair was a wild labyrinth of its own, bright red, and she lined her eyes with the ashes of the fires she used to light pages and pages of books, deep into the night, so that her eyes had a sullen quality, and when she entered the main hall at her father’s calling, Theseus was driven wild at her eyes and her hair and called to her that he had come to slay the Minotaur and her laugh sounded to him like the twinkling of glass, which he mistook as fragility. Minos saw this and knew that it was good and felt comfort, some hope that this confident young man would bring her out of the depths through which she was wading. His love for her was a desperation, because he didn't know
When Theseus met Ariadne she knew exactly who he was and saw through him in knowing this; she saw his confidence and his willingness and she felt the dark pool of Night that lived within her stir and beckon, so she listened and cracked a smile that was every bit that which had pulled Theseus across the ocean, the smile of an open snowy expanse yawning siren-like when the cold is too much, and Theseus followed the flame in his own eyes and was in love, and she knew this."

The embers crackled, I hugged my blanket in closer, and the moon's sliver cast no light upon us. The cowboy continued .

"She sent for him to follow her to her chambers and in those chambers she gave him a golden thread, she looked him in the eyes and said 'I give to you that which I believe I should be mde from' and told him that with the thread he could find his way back to her, that his love for her would be proven,"
"He looked upon her with her eyes that plumbed his depths and he believed her, that if only to conquer the minotaur would she be conquered for himself"

For to Ariadne the labyrinth was to sacrifice souls into the story of exploration as though to make a point of what it means to seek too much of the unknown. It was this fearful knowledge that she held within herself to which she held a flame of her reading fires each night; that she need avoid some corners of her own thoughts for fear they would swallow her as the labyrinth would swallow those she commanded her father to sacrifice into it, and their disappearance was confirmation if nothing else, that you should not explore that which you are not equipped to understand.
