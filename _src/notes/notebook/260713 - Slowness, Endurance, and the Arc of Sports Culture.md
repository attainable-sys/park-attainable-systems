Life gives us coincidences as a way of making sure we are paying attention. Probably, anyway. Only if you're paying attention.
Náko has a great bench outside front of the place - it doesn't shift when someone sits down to share its length with you, which means that if you're in the middle of writing a sentence by hand, there's little risk of their sitting down messing with your handwriting. I got back from Seattle on Saturday on another redeye, and because it took me all weekend to recover from the bad decision of thinking I can replace sleep with travel, I only got to my usual coffee spot in the neighborhood today.
Three people sat down next to me today, but one stood out because I thought I knew him -- I don't say anything at first because I was in the midst of writing, but glancing over his shoulder while writing a sentence (as one does) I see that his email signature matched his hat, and it confirms for me likely we'd met in another life. I tell him so as he got up to leave.
The person who sat down next to me turns out to be Ben of Attaquer. After trying to figure out the overlap, we confirm that he lived in Brooklyn around the same time that the cycling boom happened.
Ben and I talk, briefly, about the rhythm of change and what is expected of people when they take part in sports, then how they defy the odds. I realize while speaking that what humans want are human experiences, and that money flows where people want to go, not when people are told they should. I get the sense that cycling somewhat died off when the sport evolved toward the inspirational instead of aspirational; when cycling athletes pushed the limits of the sport, the sport began to feel less accessible, and less people *aspired* to be a part of it. Attaquer was founded in 2012 in Sydney, and I must have met Ben when he lived Brooklyn. It's not clear either of us really remembered one another, but we were both here for that particular period of time where New York City was the epicentre of the cycling world, so there was common ground to chat over.
I invite him into my thoughts; he tells me that more than a few people he knows who moved from cycling to the running world are rotating back to two wheels. He's seen cycling change, he ran marathons since it did, and now cycling seems to be coming back. There was a time before running was the sport du jour, and I've been meaning to talk, a little, about what it looked like when everyone collectively moved through that trend, as well why it is that running is going through the same arc right now. I really don't want it to, but it's likely the way of trends.
A few years ago, I ran the White River 50km ultra outside of Tacoma, WA with a close friend. At one point in the run, we stopped on a ridge with Rainier standing up ahead, and others passed, asking us if something was wrong. "There's a mountain!" I exclaimed, pointing at the looming presence of the thing. "Oh, yeah," many of them said. I suspect many of them were from Washington or the surrounding states, where it is normal to see massive quantities of stone and earth protruding into the sky. It is something I tell myself I would take for granted if I lived there, even while I'd hope I wouldn't. Everything becomes normal after a while.
However, I tell myself that this is what I am learning from running: that if I learn to stay strong, I can also learn to endure the longer effort, and I can enjoy it for longer. The journey is sweeter specifically because with endurance, it becomes easier to enjoy the sights along the way rather than barreling headlong into the effort and then looking back with the broken sense that you don't remember any of it.
I don't know if the same can be said for careers. My immediate sense is that others take on the ambition of careening toward the completion of an effort — they miss a lot. Attaquer celebrated a 10-year run as a brand a few years ago, according to a quick google, which means that the brand survived the bust of trend popularity that followed cycling. Maybe they end up happy,--I don't know. I just don't want to get to whatever the next stage is and realize I didn't pay enough attention to how I got there. I don't know if it's the same for careers, but I do think that those who take a long, slow, meandering path through the woods are probably those who know it better than anyone else, and they are probably happier in the end for it — they saw the moss growing on a fallen tree that will one day become soil; they paid attention to the ferns that grow from the upturned roots — they can see the mountain and so much more in the midst of moving forward. People who stick with a single job without the need to rush ahead and get to the very top of it all do indeed seem to sustain their appreciation for what they are doing.
I also think there are a lot of ways to frame a 'career' - we have careers that make us money, but then we have careers that act more like vocations. There's an expression in spanish I sit with quite often. *A que te dedicas?* Most people would translate it directly to say that it means it's own translation: what do you do for a living, but I like to sit on the edges of the definition, because directly translated it means 'what are you dedicated to'?
Maybe the reason running is like meditation stems from this fact: compelled by forward movement in a steady rhythm, we find ourselves able to sit within our thoughts. For a long time I envied people who said they would think when they ran. I often said that it was when I thought the least. Now I realize I had never tried to. Running, paradoxically, has given me the tools to slow down. Now, and only now, years into the practice, do I have some of the tools necessary to pay attention and sift through what is at the center of what I'm thinking. For a long time, what dictated what happened was only whether I was moving, not whether or not I was actually doing what I intended to. The conscientiousness of running only comes under the expectation that the movement is the point, and certainly not at the velocities expected of this world.
I wonder aloud if it's just because they want to be outside again. It's the same reason rock climbing will once again have its moment, and I wonder if this happens because we do actually know what we need most. What I want for either of these sports is the sense that people will actually respect the world that gives them so much. I've been seeing a lot more of Ultraromance lately, probably for this reason. Ronnie Romance, or 'Ultraromance' as he was initially made popular through the instagram cycling era, is a cyclist who has always focused on 'anti-Fred' culture. People want to romanticize the experience of enjoying something, and when this is sapped from the experience itself. I think people can tell. A small, optimistic part of me wants to believe that maybe this time it will be different, and people will remember that it is the outdoor they are after — that they will pause for a moment to actually see the mountains in front of them. Maybe this time they'll remember that the air we breathe was paid for by the trees they run or they walk past.
