Oer the weekend, Lauren and I went on a long walk over to the part with Iro and talked about how I'm feeling. She said she thinks I do know what I should be doing, but that there is fear ties up in the feeling of taking a risk, in trying to get my writing in front of people -- possible to be judged for the content of the writing itself. It's hard to say -- but I do I think I fear committing to the task of putting my efforts toward writing as something I do well. I don't particularly know if what is buried beneath my works is worth reading, of it even the content on the surface means anything at all. This is the critical point to be made -- we have moved so far toward a topical interpretation of what needs to be said in order for it to be 'relevant' that the contemporaneous effort is all we can pay attention to . It's all we really let count.
This means that when we write (at least in the sense that we write in order to be read by others), we write only about the present. There seems to be very little historicity in this act - a lack of attention to the time in which it happens and all the times that precede it: we write chiefly about the present. There seems to be very little historicity in to the manner in which today's writers now approach thinking about the present - it seems many writers are unconsciously obsessed with the theme of relevance, instead, and not to write about the present is to miss the effects of one's writing on the future and as a reference the future will use from the past in order to build better futures. The only thinkers I see referenced today are those considered 'classics', whose experience of the world seems so far removed from the events of the present as to feel as though it can only be an analogue in abstract -- pattern matching with no attempt at historicity.
Where this bothers me most is in the effect this has on how we might resolve the events of the present - they do not exist in this abstract space with which modern discourse treats the past, more as a set of legends than a set of progressive events leading toward the present. We live in unsettled times, but it truly does not feel as though much is being done to stop them. I saw a reddit thread from r/greenland the other day speaking gon the relative inaction of the American public on the front of unrest against what nearly all people agree is an aggressive and oppressive use of force against neighbords and civilians - it is not enough for someone to say that "that's not us, we're sorry" to the oppressed because it doesn't actually change anything. I myself am apathetic to the effects of protest as anything more than a solidarity-making event. It doesn't feel, to me, that they actually cause change, rather they exist as an event to reify the ambient shared opinion of all those who participate in it. I don't think it ends up changing the power structures in question. I do wonder if the reason I feel this way comes from either an instituted logic toward effectiveness from systems of power, or if it truly is the case that modern protest is either ineffective or neutered. If the only way protest in the modern age can be effective is through the thread of violence, then maybe that's what is lacking.
{note here, I do not mean or condone violence, but I do understand that the threat of it is a powerful cultural tool - the importance of this symbolism is largely what I am gesturing toward}
The January 6 riot on the US capital is a great example that maybe is instructive of what I'm indicating. Objective ofwhet er or not the people in question were truly riotous or 'patriots' in the sense that they have been declaimed, their effectiveness is in the message that violence is a political tool. The act itself was a threat of violence acted upon that did indeed violently cost one life and harmed many others.
The reality of what this does to the fabric of credibility toward the effectiveness of modern protest causes us to think that the largely benign pink-hat MeToo marches of 2017 and the violence of the January 6th protests represent drastically different paths of action. I can't speak to the effectiveness of the MeToo movement other than to echo what Solnit says about victory -- that it often doesn't feel like anything happened at all. [[Solnit]] says this specifically in reference to 'environmental victories', saying that "most {...} victories look like nothing happened."
I find myself wondering if the right dopaminergic loops of an attention-strained world make it so we can't reconcile the progressive victories of the last 20 or so years. Truthfully, we have made tremendous social progress in so many dimensions that it is difficult to account for all of them - the arc of progressive thought from the 60s into the modern moment has meant that the world (yes, the world, with America as the cultural center of this last 60 year arc) has largely become more tolerant of difference. But it is true to say that these victories are insufficient and that there is so much more to be done. It would be wrong to say that 'enough' has progressed and I am militantly skeptical of anyone claiming that we have "gone too far" in a more tolerant, compassionate politics. I'd also be the first to say that many of these dimensions may have resulted in some rather strange corners of human expression coming into public view in the hopes that they be included - but I cannot say that if it is harmful to others, these expressions should ever be denied their right.
However, the question of including historicity in modern commentary on social progress and discourse (what little there seems to be other than in select fast-loop shortform video opinions hosted on the platforms of corporations that hope you'll hop over to the next video before you take long enough to form your own response to what you've just watched) is chiefly a question of storytelling. We live in a story-filled time, with so many avenues through which to communicate with the with the notion of action and victory - but these victories are difficult to translate into an encouragement to keep going. The triumph of action toward creating better conditions for people are "triumphs invisible except through storytelling" (Solnit again), and the boundaries of memory make is so that even were there to be a discussion on the facts of a present encouraged encouraged by the trimph of a progressive agent a, it is lost in the rapid tick of content to content, of subject to subject. We do not pay attention long enough for a story to affect us in the first place.
I can't say I know what the solution is, but I do know that the present we live in is one that seems continuously to flicker with the light of change. It is not all good change. The axis along which most recent disruptions in the global state seem to have progressed is mostly not a good one. It has mostly not helped people. It is mostly oppressive, or at best a grift. I can also say that despite all of this negative, oppressive force, it is the case that we *have* seen progress in my lifetime and in that of the people who raised me. We don't celebrate this sort of victory because the progress feels at best incremental and more often than not is simple a case of ensuring that things are as they should have been to start with; in most cases a politic that affords maximal degrees of opportunity along with a drive to decrease degrees of oppression feels simply as it should be: mundane, and uneventful. It doesn't feel like victory because winning the war in this particular dimension means letting people live normal lives. Presentism reinforces this fact in knowing that oppressed people being offered the chance to live normal lives will look like nothing is happening,-- the historicity of victory will be forgotten in the fact that freedom won does not feel like much at all but the right to live one's life without fear of one's live being threatened by the act of doing so.
It is also the reason that people from an oppressed group might at some point forget the fight that was won and take it for granted. I say, let them forget -- this is victory. It is not to say that it should be forgotten or that the past should not be honored, but if there are pockets of the formerly oppressed that have the privilege of forgetting the past that brought them their freedom, then we truly have progressed, and there is something to be proud of in this act.
