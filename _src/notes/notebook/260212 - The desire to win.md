Several months ago, I saw the movie F1 with my partner - and I came out of it with the spirit of victory: I came out of it with the desire to win. In the wake of this, I wrote it in my notebook -- I intend to become the optimal version of myself that "wins" in the dimensions I care about most.
Interestingly, what has happened since then has not necessarily been falling into a 'grindset' or pulling myself toward the 'ideal routine'. Instead, I have taken several leaping steps back to reconsider how I want to approach my own self improvement and in asking myself specifically *where* I want to win. I have never been particularly motivated by money; I am far more enticed by the prospect of growing in new and unexpected ways. If you know me, this is nothing new, and it's something that has run through my experiences - I have always cast for the biggest effort with the highest stakes in order to see the most outsized results. Paradoxically, people who don't really care about money and care about their lives in this way seem to be what our economy favors, at the highest level - the 'vanguard personality' is one who those face with intractable problems can place on the front line of reasoning through these problems. The byproduct of this is that I have enjoyed a career of transformation in the face of wicked problems, with lots of space for creativity, and that I have been paid to do so. I do not know many people who have this privilege.
I do however reflect on the value that this sort of work has brought me: on the one side I have had the uncommon experience of working with frontier technologies, or in working through problems that express themselves at a global scale. I have found this work incredibly fruitful and rewarding.
However, one glaring issue has continued to plague any progression in the conclusive actions I've taken; I think that when we are made to believe that our actions must take effect at the global scale, we're made to believe in their imperative at the cost of our own well-being and growth. We believe that we are responsible for the world, and often in a way that pollutes our ability to understand that we ourselves possess our own internal worlds. When these are left to fallow in the face of problems much larger than who we are, we lose perspective, and we begin not to take care of ourselves.
How this has manifested for me is typical of anyone who overcommits to a problem at fross personal cost: I burned out. Hard. I lost the fire completely for the problems I was approaching, and I fell into a hateful, angry version of myself. If you know me, you likely understand that this is not my disposition. The fact that it had become such ate me alive.
What I did in response was retreat and focus more entirely on myself. Over the last six-or-so months, I have invested drastically more time into discovering who I am rather than the impression I want to leave on others; I care far more what I think about myself now than what someone else might think. I care more about how the actions I take on a daily basis result in material benefit to the immediacy of my daily experience and my own personal growth than I do the nameless thing toward which we are often oriented in the name of 'progress'. I don't write this with intent to convince people I have gone 'monk-mode' and have dived deep into an asceticism I often see glorified by others who proselytize their mission of interiority. I do however think some ascetic aspects have worked their way into the process by which I engage with each day.
I view the asceticism that some people are searching for as determination to find 'something' out., without ever really doing the work to question what exactly motivates this determination. The asceticism I seem to have found instead is far from the disciplined portrayal of repetition that often comes to mind what people think of their made up version of being a monk. It's is instead a practice of letting go, and being determined each and every day to do so. That determination, and engaging with a homonymic approach to my life has resulted in an unexpected expansion in dimensions I don't think any amount of 'locking in' could ever really produce. I have allowed myself to dream of the aesthetics of who I want to become, and in the practice of seeking out these dreams I have found the asceticism that works best for me - a pursuit of continuous joy.
In practice (more on this in a moment) it has meant one thing: taking care of my mind. Feeding it with the joy of existing, with curiosity, with fruitful adventures of interior and exterior reasoning. In the last six months, this has generated materially reasonable oucomesL: I applied to graduate schboolsl I ran another marathonl I am now writing a novel; at the backblone of all this work has been the ascetic diligence of engaging with one specific process that has been actid in support of all these outcomes: I have written, consistently, almost every single day.
The product of this is likely what anyone who writes with regularity would impart as a conclusion: I do feel better about myself, and I do feel more in charge of my life. However this is not the most startling conclusion to which I have come as a result of this practice. What instead has brought itself into the foreground through writing (to date, seven back-to-back filled notebooks) is an engagement with an interior self whose vastness I before had no way of describing in sufficient detail. It is as though a vast, yawning valley of unexplored potential and fertile earth has opened within me and my interiority has expanded to a scale I did not take the time to appreciate until I gave myself the tools to see it.
I would not claim what I write here as optimism. It is far more reasoned an motion than this. I think what I experience is more like a protracted, attentive, and yet determined suggestion to continue throught he struggle. For those who have escaped this struggle (if momentarily, in the arc of history), I think coming face-to-face with what otherwise is a common hguman experience is daunting, devastating, and terrifying. The most recent viral essay by Matt shumer is right to issue this caustion to those who otherwise had escaped the context of the human experience. It would however be wrong to say that his dictum of caution applies to everyone; the facts are that most have struggled, are struggling and will continue to struggle. What the best of those thinkers among whom struggling has been the norm have to say is ascetic: we struggle, it does not change, and we keep on through the struggle. It is a dogged determination that offers a path through but critically it is also the direction in which this determination must be aimed. You must keep going.
This is not optimism, but this is joyful. To grow, from my experience, is one of teh most joyful acts, just as to struggle together is one of the most binding. We build better societies through struggle than we have through complacency. We learn more. We become more vast.
I think this, more than anything else, is what fuels for me the determination with which I face each day: we live in some of the most dire of times, these times are dire by our own making, but times have always been dire for most, and usually at someone or other's making.
I write this I suppose bnecause somehwer ein the continuously expanding unknown of my own interiority I have found peace in my struggle. I trust that I will navigate through it. I have fcome to enjoy it, and even I have become more compassional for those who struggled before me, who continue to struggle now. To struggle is to live, no matter the struggle. The rampancy of change --its velocity, nor its degree--, does not change this. It is all struggle by varying degrees.
But in the process we must practice in seeking the means to respond to our struggle, determined as we must be to survive it, there is freedom.
