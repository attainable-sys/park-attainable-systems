There was a tree that used to fill Herbert Von King's dog park. It was a massive, hulking, old thing with branches that offered shade and divots where the roots poked up from underneath the loam and wood chips.
They cut the tree down in 2025. I'm pretty sure it was dying, watered mostly by the piss from 30 years of dogs who frequented the park. It was taller than any other tree I've seen survive those conditions. I was proud of it, and it made me smile every time I entered the park
Now the dog park has an open, larger feeling to it --by all measures getting rid of the tree opened up space for the dogs to play-- but I still miss it. I took a photograph of a couple sitting on one of the piss covered benches that used to sit in the trees shade. I sent them the photograph and got back one word of "thanks!" and felt like I got to capture something for them, under the shade of a tree that isn't there anymore.
I guess it's not really about the tree or the couple, but the empty space at the middle of the park is its own kind of memory--something so large that used to take up so much room, gone, leaving behind something new, but it's lost still hanging in the air above the park. People who never knew it was there probably don't remember it and don't miss it.
I think somewhere in going to that park after they took the tree down, I began to say goodbye to Iro. He still had years left.--I think the feeling was one of encountering the hole I knew would open up in my life and trying to steel myself against it. I hardly ever went to that park. I barely ever sat on the benches that had I had been coming to for years, for all of Iro's life, partially due to the busyness of parental neglect toward our best friends, but also because we've been coming to it his entire life and at some point it started feeling like each time was one of the last times. He was fine. He was in good health. He still ran around, he just that year had begun to speak to me more clearly.
The first day I felt like I was saying goodbye, he took me to the door of the dog park, telling me that he was ready to leave. He'd never done that before, never before getting bored and wanting to move on. When we walked out of the park, I got down on my knees, thinking about him, about how he chose to tell me what he wanted, and how I'd finally, ten years in, been learning how to listen. I took his head in my hands and spoke to him, telling him that I don't have words for the space in my heart that he fills. Usually, when I perform that sort of affection toward him, there is a sort of pulling away I feel in him, or he'll whine, wanting to keep walking. This time, he sat there, his heavy head in my arms, and he let me breathe into his fur, letting me kiss his forehead.
We started walking home, but he pulled me towards a coffee shop, one that had opened up on the edge of the park just a few years before. He pulled me towards the folding tables. It was cold, the kind of cold that holds on to the memory of winter through the water suspended in the air, the humidity increasing and threatening to jump clear past spring, into the sweating summer of Brooklyn. That was the day after the temperature had jumped up to 70°, then swung back down like a hammer. That previous day, I had walked, sweating in too many layers, between the gym and home, twice. At the coffeeshop, I parked Iro down by the tables, went inside, and as I ordered nine dollars worth of turmeric floating in nut milk, I cried behind my sunglasses, quietly sniffing as though allergic to the season. I couldn't tell why he had taken me there.
My dog has been a load-bearing tree in the canopy life for as long as he has been alive. He didn't make that choice - I didn't even get to make the choice as to whether or not it happened. An ex decided I was getting a dog, and made the choice for me, and I still have him. She didn't even send me a message to let me know when her own dog died years after we separated. I found out through a friend's instagram post. I had to unlearn a lot that I learned about training dogs after dating her - too many rules had been written and I realized, too late, they were getting in the way of closeness. He wasn't happy, and I was a bitch to him. I think it wasn't until Iro was nine that I started to actually talk to him and understand who he was. That's maybe when the branches grew a little wider above me, but it was around six that I started to fear what my life would be without him.
I started writing this essay about him because in that moment that I had been crossing the street toward that coffeeshop, I felt the intense awareness of how that missing tree felt in the park and how long we'd been coming. The same feeling is easy to simulate when you walk through the forest and a tree has fallen - the canopy has a gap in it, and the sky seems eager to flow in, but the vault of the trees overhead no longer feel like cover; we are reminded that they are just branches reaching up toward the sky, shyly avoiding one another in the canopy - they call 'crown shyness'. When the space of all those branches that don't touch is missing a piece of its puzzle, the absence is obvious, unremitting. I started writing this essay because I knew that one day that massive supporting tangle of branches that held up the sky above me would disappear, and I'd have to sit in the exposed air underneath it, and I wouldn't feel protected anymore.
I've never understood why anyone who has had a dog in their life could ever say 'it's just a dog' - my mother said it once and I felt earnestly short of slapping her. Iro was the only reason I survived being broken up with by the woman who got him for me, and maybe the only reason I survived living beyond the gap she left in my life after she left and took the dog I had helped her raise.
