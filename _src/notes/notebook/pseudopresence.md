The emotional regulation does not seem to hold - the desire to stave off anxiety around possibility and it's weight as an emissary of its own peace, all the while in a state of feeling uncommon with one's own surroundings, as though a part of something both greater and utterly uncaring of your participation.

This is the feeling brought forth by Saturdays by the feeling of having had to think about one's past in a state of motion even while we are attempting to regulate ourselves against the future and past emotions. As [[Mo Gawdat]] mentions, [[anxiety]] is a feeling that sits in the future because it is a fear of what will happen next. [[Happiness]] is instead found in the present, in the happiness found after recognizing what is in your control and withholding fear that desires to bloom forth through any meaningful consideration of the past and future as features of our lives we wish we could change, and it is only in the present that we can truly take action.

So it is a question to me why [[fear]] envelops me, why in this moment it is an emotional state that I'm experiencing - and I'm forced to believe that it is a product of [[anxiety]] of looking forward and seeing that any set of outcomes are available, any set of outcomes might happen. So we work through the exercise and ask the question - if the fear is that Im going to get off of this train and hear that something devastating has happened, is it real? It is not - it is not something that I need to experience, and because of this is something that I can tell my brain not to experience, it's something that I can push to the side because it is not real. I am in charge of this feeling and I am in charge of what it constitutes.

---

Recently, I've been listening to [[Mo Gawdat]] - the former CBO of Google who, since the passing of his son at 21, turned his life toward the pursuit of happiness for as many people as possible. In particular, I was listening to a conversation he held with Steven Barlett ([Diary of a Ceo](https://open.spotify.com/episode/1dBnMUeFd0nqX6j4q7KQK5?si=fd19b55b4a844727)) - where [[Mo Gawdat|Gawdat]] refers to the time-based notion of emotion. His claim is that most negative emotions are couched in the future and the past as either symptoms of regret or expectation, and that the unhappiness that comes with negative thinking often emerges through an interaction with that which we can't change.

This has had me thinking about the general rise in anxiety[^1] in present times that seems linked to a well-connected society's general inability to exist in the present - we are in many places at once, but we are never in any of these places because technology is not a substitute for being physically and psychically located in a place and a time. Rather than moving with [[Mo Gawdat|Gawdat]]'s definition of happiness being a function of that which you cannot change and a lack of imperative to exist in the present moment, I would exact the definition to have it describe a need to be in the _physical_ present. More explicitly, I find that when I'm most anxious while using technology, it's because I am interacting with a _present_ I cannot directly affect.

In [[Mo Gawdat]]'s definition of anxiety or regret, the temporal location of negative emotions pull one away from the present, and the solution for this is to direct one's attention toward the present moment. It's not something that comes easily to people, but it's a meditation on the feeling of knowing - the feeling of examining possibility as something that is interesting, but not that is determinate, because only those things that lie either outside or inside of out control are those that we can think about. Anything else is imaginary and exists outside of those bounds, they are features of existence that reach out for attention but are not tangible; you cannot touch them, you cannot feel them.

This is the great mistake that has arisen with creating technologies that exist to reconstitute our presences in several spaces at once. We even call video calling 'telepresence', as though there is a feature of this sort of interaction that constitutes meaningful interaction. Telepresence is likely better termed 'pseudopresence', enveloping a construct of terms that describe the ability to experience anxiety around one's need to exist and affect several places at once. It's an experience that is not tangible and not real, because at best our ability to telegraph the appearance of presence must be acted upon locally by those who are physically in a location and capable of affecting local reality.

This may be the reason for a grand state of deflation washing over us when we get off of yet another 'telepresence' call with someone, and likely equally the reason that despite the rhetoric of liberty desired by workers, there is still an underlying desire among the lonely public to interact with other humans. We need touch because we need to be reminded that we have bodies. We might not want to spend all of our days under the panopticon of controlling leadership that treats us an implement of its desires--this impinges on the human desire for autonomy--but we do still desire interacting with one another. Any form of interaction that takes place with full engagement of both the mind and body interacting with something that is physically present is a predicate for having felt as though we can truly affect our environment. We do not feel this when we have 6 hours of telepresence calls because being 'telepresent' is not the same and being present, else it would be given the same name.

While there is an anxiety that exists when sitting with a fear of the future and a regret that couches itself in the past, [[pseudopresence]] also places us in a state of anxiety about an inability to interact with and affect the present, inherently leaning us toward a state of forward-leaning anxiety as we sit on the expectation of what should be done next rather than the ability to affect it directly. In the most austere of emotional circumstance, imagine being on a video call while watching someone experience the worst damage of their life - we are utterly unable to prevent the reality of the present that is happening on the other end of the call, and are as a result horrified by the reality that out anxiety is laid bare.

As just one symptom of a general anxiety that seems to exist around the use of technology to exist in several places at once, I'm convinced this is the reason that telepresence is so difficult for actually feeling a genuine connection with the people you speak to. Most information technology that concerns itself with informing people about the present is doing so at the cost of habing people understand that the present they are being informed on is not one that they can directly affect. This is the product of millions of news feeds, media consumption, and yes, telepresence that produces this experience.

It's also not to say that we should broadly define telepresence as negative - it does not wholly remove of us all ability to have meaningful interactions over digital media. I have cried over text messages and phone calls, I have laughed heartily in the chat, I have been in teletherapy for years - but to depend wholly on this form of interaction as equivalent to what happens in the physical realm feels like an austere rejection of the somatic experience - we have many other senses and they work together to characterise our experience.

However It's not to say that you can't; I have had my share of telepresence calls that are meaningful and have actively been engaged in telepresence therapy for about 4 years now - but I'm aware of the truth that an anxiety sitting atop our experience of the present will always remove of us the ability to experience what we are doing at the moment that we are doing it. Our calendars are backed up entirely with back-to-back telepresence such that by the time one conversation ends, we immediately commence another call with a completely different context, starting with the same phrase of 'where are you based?' out of a desire to place the stranger in a physical context.

If [[Mo Gawdat|Gawdatt]] is right, attempting to '[solve for happy](https://www.mogawdat.com/solve-for-happy)' becomes a question of navigating the utility of technology in our hyperconnected present - developing an understanding of pseudopresence is probably a powerful tool for appreciating modern anxiety in the context of social interaction. Our understanding of modern anxiety and social interaction can be explained by the effect of the technologies that inform it. While technology offers us the illusion of being everywhere at once, it simultaneously robs us of the ability to be fully present anywhere. It's not just about being mentally present, but also about prioritizing physical presence when possible, and fully engaging in our digital interactions when necessary.

Instead of rejecting the presence of these phenomenae, we're forced to learn how to navigate their presence consciously by reorganizing our reaction to the limitations they impose and the effects technology have on our ability to be present. Only then can we hope to mitigate an anxiety arising from being everywhere and nowhere at once.
