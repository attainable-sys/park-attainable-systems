It is so very strange to me how it is that we can be pulled from the energy that we had at one moment in time, directly into having completely been lost from the energy within us in order to write. Chiefly I find that inspiration does have a sort of mania to it - an excitement for what is touched upon, but with a loss accompanying the realization that there is finite time within which we as individuals are capable of utilizing the energy that we have at out disposal.

[[Rachael]] does point out very well that it seems my chief fear is time, that I am always trying to buy it back through one mean or other, because I don't wnat to run out of time to explore the thoughts that I want to engage. I can see that there's something true there. It's possible that the writing I want to do concerns itself with a fear of understanding that time is relative, but that we still age despite it, and that while a fear of death is something I believe we should all come to terms with, it's still a fear that the time you have is cut short or is badly used.

The memories we hold are those that will carry us the most into the futures we want to live - those that we have chosen to make important to us, those that we believe are something more than the sum of their parts and can be understood to matter to our constitution of self. If I remember the time I spent at that cabin in the woods so fondly, what is the reason? I didn't want to talk to [[Ruby Welkovich|Ruby]] at the time, but I did end up doing so several times, likely out of a desire to stave off the loneliness that crept up when I wasn't being attentive enough toward the solitude that I was seeking.

There are three inciting events that we can point to in that experience, that I was there to witness the eclipse, that I was there to be by myself and didn't get to enjoy this outcome when [[Henry de Valence|Henry]] showed up, and finally the missile silo with which I'm some what obsessed with wanting to explore, chiefly because the scale of the structure is so massive that I can't think of any reason not to want to explore it in its entirety (things hidden below the surface beg a sense of intrigue - I took photos on that day that I never did anything with, and I should change that). My sense is that if there is any story to be told here, its around the knowledge of the missile silo's implications and the complete awe with which I received the total solar eclipse - a contrast between that which is known and the terror of knowing this truth.

I sat down at a formica table in a trailer somewhere upstate, a place whose name I don't really remember and whose restaurants I didn't actually order food from, but I went there in the latter of half of winter on a search for enough inspiration to fold around the writing that I had driven up there to do. I don't know what voice should be employed to write about this, speaking about it in past tense feels a bit lost in the intention and I'm not sure that I want to that that approach, because I think there is likely far more that I need to explore in the writing. Maybe the way to approach it is in present tense rather than past - or even some sort of future imperfect, as though a reader is being pulled through the text that is being written.

I'm not sure how I could approach it, I'm struck by the fact that writing is something that comes naturally to me, but that it has never actually been focused into a form of effort that I can call comprehensive--there are satellites of ideas that float around execution, but never anything final--and these incomplete bodies make up a desire to create something bigger without ever actually getting to the core matter at hand, or a desire to encourage a reader through a body of work that anyone can call 'complete'. This is probably what I fear the most - that I've never put the concerted effort into finishing something so that there's actually something for people to hold on to.

I think this is why I want to get into the practice of actually creating writing that people consume and which they can offer feedback about - unless this is simply not how writing works.

> Write a journal entry from the perspective of a character named "Echo," who is struggling to find her place in the world. Echo has always felt like she's been living in someone else's shadow, whether it's her parents' expectations or her best friend's accomplishments.

While Maura and I grew up together, it was around fifteen when I started to feel more frustration than not about how she was able to move through the world - the fact that she seemed to be able to engage people in a way that I always feared doing, something that I wasn't really equipped to do, or that she seemed far more capable than I was in just about every category of teenage achievement. I was always trailing behind her, whether following her in plans to the mall, planning her birthday parties, or acting as a neutral third party to the friendships-cum-relationships she seemed itinerant in avoiding.

In hindsight, it's obvious to anyone beyond the age of sixteen that insecurity is a core feature of young adulthood, and this was something that fueled Maura's spotlight - she thrived in being seen, but being seen was effortless. The flywheel of her visibility reverberated, but the anxiety that surrounded being seen and needing to keep up appearances always turned up in the conversations we held in my bedroom, her mother driving by later most evenings to pick her up. It was slow at first, but the envy that my association with her was causing me to seem less important drove a wedge between us, and I was the first to notice its presence; I needed time where I was allowed to take up room and be seen.

It's worse that her problems were so much more interesting than mine. She fell in love when she was sixteen with a nineteen-year-old who worked at a local pizza shop and we found so many reasons as a result to frequent the dusty formica and weathered red roof of a restaurant whose menu never changed, whose crusts were always as crispy as the first time, and whose root beer was always flat. The boy still seemed somehow oblivious to her advances; by most accounts she was more attractive than he was, but Maura wasn't a simpleton - her tendency toward introspection afforded an admirable sensitivity to character. Tolin *was* interesting - he liked science fiction novels and knew about cars, and often when he was the only one in the pizza shop, the three of us would sit for ours exploring the worlds he knew about - but Maura never seemed to be able to hint at him that she was actually interested in him beyond the friendship that we shared, and the duvet-cover debriefs that followed these nights often focused on how to overcome the issue, but never actually found resolution. I also never felt motivated to solve it for her.

I could see that over time I was starting to make myself smaller, that I wasn't growing from the process of being her friend. I was stunting myself. It was a difficult conversation to have when she finally came to me, concerned that I was distant because (finally turning her attention toward me) something was going on in my life.

There was, I had to explain to her. My life was happening. I was seeking to have one that existed outside of her, outside of a friendship that seemed otherwise all-consuming. I was glad for her achievements, but they were always hers, I and I needed my own. The crestfallen look on her face when I told her that I'd already arranged for my mother to speak with hers and ask that they come up with other after-school plans so as to free up my time for myself was a pivot - a moment where I realized my own self-conception and the spark of self-worth swelled inside of me at my own defense. I loved her dearly; I needed my own life.
