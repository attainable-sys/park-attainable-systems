Started realizing yesterday in my conversation with Patrick that I actually know so much more about networks than I realize - which really has me reeling. There's a split in definition between what a network is and what a system is, and what most people mean when they say network is actually system. This being true, I'm not sure it's difficult to re-orient public definition toward the latter, but I could see it being possible given the correct set of definitions placed in motion as a result of the work that goes into it. That's really the best way I can think to explain it, any sufficiently reductive explanation without at the very least analogous context eventually results in a description that is so coarse-grained as to seem unintelligible and requiring additional description. That's just one of those things. In any case, there's a lot of thinking there that is worth exploring, especially when considering that some of the top thinkers in the space are still navelgazing over the possibility that a path-dependent course of analysis is the way to examine things like community, so being able to generalize a science of systems from first principles is at least in some ways a valid pursuit, especially when considering the possibility that a sufficiently developed science would be one in which the thinking that comes from it can provide a framework to re-think how it is that we consider the growth, stability, and death of systems as they are. Pursuing something along these lines is a valid analysis and can be done neutrally in order to recognize that at some point it requires an appreciation of more remote concepts such at negative and positive externalities and carrying capacity of a system - the notion of cannibalization or prey-predator dynamics as characteristic of a system are easy to zoom out from if you understand to a sufficient degree what is actually needed in order for a system to exist. So what are these fundamental rules of system design, I wonder - how much have they actually been thought out, and what kind of information is actually necessary in order for a system to exist successfully? There's an interesting question there around sufficiency - what is the smallest unit of lossless information in a system? It should in theory be possible for a system to exist without needing above a certain degree of fidelity necessary in order to function. Coarse-grain information in certain bounds is probably sufficient in most contexts, but I do wonder what might be needed when it comes to the specifics, i.e. are there scenarios in which the complex work that is done at one level needs to be shared in all of its complexity at the next in order to function? At some degree this will require trust between parties and becomes a game theory question within the bounds of a system occupied by autonomous agents. How often is this actually the case? I think in most systems, the autonomy is bounded - all we mean by autonomy in the contexts where it is necessary is the the agents can choose to or not to participate in the particular topology of interaction necessary for their role or scope of interaction. It makes me think about how the role of an analyst is actually to act as an information summary mechanism - something whose role is actively to fold through the effort necessary to synthesize information that exists within a system and reduce it to that which is necessary to disseminate. In this context, even though there was a large amount of work done in the components of the system, the amount of information necessary for success between parties and/or tiers of a system does not require this level of fidelity, i.e. this is the reason that an executive summary for a report is sufficient for most contexts, or why it is that an electrician who knows which screw to turn is a valid synthesis of their experience. Both are equally valid ways through which information is being moved through a system, because the work that is being done at one level is being compressed down to a single action that is relevant to the next level. That's an interesting realization - most information passed between systems is actually compressed in order for the system to function. Is this true in nature? Do we have lossless communication between components? Is there actually an analogue? The first thing that comes to mins is the possibility that there's some sort of compression between what a part of an ecosystem feels and what it communicates to the rest of the system; a tree that has fallen ill can use a mycorrhizal network to communicate a threat to the rest of the system, but the way that it does so is through a set of chemical triggers that cause a basic set of responses by the rest of the system.
