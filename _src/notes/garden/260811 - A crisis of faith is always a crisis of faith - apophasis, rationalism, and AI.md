- Emphatic, but empathetic. I do not think mathematics is over, and while the scream that we must be feeling is the realization that a profound change must happen, some part of us must as a species have known it was coming, just as we have had to experience that same despair before.

I read [an article](https://substack.com/home/post/p-208406391) recently that describes what is happening in mathematics in response to AI. The writing is beautiful, poetic and despairing, and I feel deep empathy for a writer and a mathematician who is navigating the ground having shifted beneath his feet. The despair of knowing that things might change and not knowing exactly how one must adapt to the shifting present is a state of, in many cases, pure terror. In this moment of human history, we are particularly *there* . I've been pursuing something recently that I think is rendering me immune to the pessimism that comes with it, or rather inured to it, because I just can't bring myself to believe that thinking is over.
I recently spoke to a man on the street who stopped me to ask for money. He was sitting in a push walker, and his shirt was clean, as were his socks. Being stopped for money happens all the time in New York, and when you have lived here long enough, it can become easy to shrug off the interaction and keep walking, however callous that makes us, but there are occasionally those who it is clear did not want to be where they are (no one does), and so I stopped, and we had a conversation.
His name was Theodore (name changed), and he had only become unhoused in the last 4 days. It was the 4th of August, and he'd been evicted. He said he didn't know what to do, he didn't have a way to make money, he was 56 and the world felt like it had moved on from him. He was trying to get $85 for the shelter for the next 3 weeks so that he would have somewhere to sleep.
There is something more obvious in those who are telling the truth of their desperation - they are not practiced in telling their story, so they stumble over it as they tell it. There is no order to what they are saying. Theodore didn't, and so I told him I was going to the store to get flowers, and I'd come back with some money I'd gotten from an ATM at the store. As I was walking, I was racking my brain for all the responses I could have had. It could have been easy not to turn back to him, but I remembered something he had said when we were standing. He said that he knew that God had a plan for him, even if it didn't make sense right now, but he wanted to have faith.
When I walked back to him, I had the twenty dollars in my hand. I'd had it when I spoke to him, but in a sense I needed to think through what to say to him. Money does not go as far as it used to, I don't have much of it, but when I got to him, I handed him the bill and I asked him when he'd last gone to church. He looked up at me and thought, saying that it had been a long time. I looked him in the eyes and I said 'Theodore, if God has a plan for you, then you should go to church, and be around those who can help you understand it.' He looked at me for a moment and I felt a sincerity pass through him, so I went on, asking if he knew a church he could go to. He said no, he asked me if there was one I knew. I told him that I don't have that relationship with what people call God, but if he did, that was where he needed to be, and I then said it again, that he needed to go to church.
My relationship with belief is very personal. I feel as though I have spent a majority of my life in one or another sort of doubt, casting off from the shores of certainty as soon as I've found it, and then frustrated as I am tossed in with the flotsam of self-discovery. I think the reason I have continually pushed off when I find certainty is because while certainty can feel safe, I'm never safe on those grounds. I grew up moving. I don't trust the ground beneath my feet.
My experience of learning to live with my own uncertainty has been more or less the mission of the last decade, and I've had to take large leaps of faith in order to do what I have, whether in my career or in my personal life. I like to think that it has offered me a bevy of tools to use when I'm encountering new insecurity.
Ideological shifts are profoundly disturbing, and in the secular (maybe post-secular?) age I find opportunity abounding in seeing that a babel-like structure can generate everything. It is true - if the library of Babel were real, would we give up our pursuits in trying to write our stories (or, more darkly, to find the story of our lives within it?). The adversarial potential of a technology that can finish our thoughts still does not have them for us. Mathematicians have already gone through this. Hilbert, Gödel, and Leibniz did this handily.
A shift in how we think cannot decide where we are going, what we are doing with our lives, and how we care about the world around us. The entire exercise of knowledge remains apophatic, and the further we hare reached toward the unknowable, the more divine that unknowable. An aesthetics prizing the use of a self-aware mind is a means by which we embody our own cosmologies, and it is a requirement for any age. It is especially true in this one. The ineffable is something we work to describe because we do not know it. Creating a mechanism to better explore those gaps is an opportunity, both of exegesis in our priors but as well to understand where if at all there is so much more yet to know.
The despair in the world around the use of AI is a bit perplexing because I don't understand how this is different from any other time in history where a discovery, a process, a way of thinking has declared thinking and progress over. It instead reveals a deep lack of teleological foundation with which the pure rationality of the modern age has left us. What I am speaking to here is that a crisis of faith is always a crisis of faith. We have known what these look like many times over, and we are still here.
I don't mean to be maudlin, dismissive or reductive - but I do think that the faithless are often the first to throw up their hands at whatever defies their belief. Understand me - it is possible not to have faith and to believe something. They are not the same.
One thing I have always enjoyed is that the best mathematicians and physicists are indeed wonderful writers. Hampshire is one such example. Strogatz is another. Rovelli writes with incredible poetry. If anything, the very fact that exploring thinking as though it is an exegetical process is particularly irrational - since the first thing many come to realize when deepening their course of study is how steadily the map can grow, despite it only ever approximating the territory. It is not all knowable, much to the chagrin of Aquinas - but it is beautiful nonetheless, and the joy of continuing to probe the dark is exactly the sort of joy that comes from discovery.

I feel for rationalists who find themselves in a position of terror at the realization that a worldview founded entirely upon rationalist foundations might have uneven footing. I'd stake the claim that many (all) of those who have claimed themselves as rationalists have sought to bound themselves within the constructs of faith to start with. Rationality allows us to shoo away uncertainty in pursuit of knowing, and when you get very good at rationalizing, it can begin to feel as though all is knowable. Any crisis precipitates despair. But an exegesis of rationalism is not a finished problem, and if those who would call themselves rationalists would pause momentarily from judging the 'irrational' (we in the humanities have had to bear this judgment a long time), it becomes possible to recognize that exegesis continues to spawn new systems of belief. Those exerting hyper-rational ideology might realize that there are, indeed, many ways to interpret the truth for its use as a construct of telos.
Now, it could be that just because I've spent most of my life calling myself an atheist that I'm not surprised that we are where we are now - there are claims to be made in and about faith in many dimensions, both ideologically as well as anthropologically, but technologically the concept is all the more interesting. What is the use of faith as a tool, and by what mechanism did it emerge for use by people as a way to survive living? Is it an aberration of the conscious mind? I could not possibly think so.
I find faith not only important, but necessary, and what we see in the pursuit of any discipline that becomes one's own purpose is the pursuit of the aforementioned telos - the way of providing meaning to what is being done. It is the reason for anyone who speaks passionately about that which they find beautiful and fascinating in the world to do so with that specific passion. There is something in and beyond the feeling of certitude, because there is also the thrill and joy of unfolding the world around us, interacting with it, and coming into contact with whatever we have come many times throughout history to call the divine. We can call it whatever we like. That little bit of programming in our brains and bodies feels good when we interact with the world, and it feels even better when we feel as though we are meant to interact with it. I do not think that it can be taken from us, especially - only that there are new ways of doing so.

One way of seeing has now changed - but of anything that can be made certain there is one in particular deserving of the most attention; there is still yet more to learn. I don't think it is necessarily a 'surprise' to learn this, as a non-mathematician - because whatever reasoning we have developed as a species cannot do more than we use it for. Language is a construct, as is mathematics. Its foundations are not real, in the explicit sense, however heretical it is to make this claim. Everything that we model is an approximation of what we actually experience. It may be because I know enough about mathematics to actually appreciate that about half of mathematics feels like an exegetical exercise of something being read for the first time - of reaching into the fabric of our surroundings and revealing what it's made of, if even they are rules - but I believe it's also because we have been relearning how to evaluate our existence for millennia now, and we continue to make progress in every dimension other than how to actually appreciate where we are.
