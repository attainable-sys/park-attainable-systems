## On the uncommon and ADHD:

In unfamiliar environments, we feel arrested by how [[uncommon]] each sensation is - it's a way to inject novelty into a brain that craves adapting to that novelty. This is the reason that traveling feels so good to someone with ADHD - it shortcuts all of the emotions that come with what is predictable. What doesn't feel good is when things are *too much* the same, because the predictability of an environment is part of what keeps it exciting. The uncommon in this way becomes a way to throw oneself into that state of [[adaptation]] in order to feel more grounded.

Oddly, it's the desire to have only what you want to work be predictable that affords the strongest sense of alignment with the self. When what is under your [[control]] can be modulated in the ways that you feel best you have to adapt to, they become anchors in the day of a person who does not otherwise feel as though they have control over their life. It reduces the amount of effort needed to make decisions, so that only the most important decisions count, meaning that you are optimized for making those decisions.

The uncommon, however, can also be a place of comfort, it can be a place where there is indeed freedom found in the pursuit of a new resting state, a desire to try out a new routine, or rather, not to have one at all and respond entirely to an environment. It would be wrong to believe that the uncommon is something that we should fear - it is a place of great discovery and ingenuity.

The tension between the uncommon and the common lies within how we engage with the common - the common is a set of parameters that we can place on the self, allowing us to run through whatever basic set of tasks and approaches we want to take on, and weigh them against that which we have no choice but to adapt to. The common is a refuge from the weight of these experiences, in order to give us a sense of continuous relative peace.

By contrast, the uncommon that which forces a person into a more animal state - allowing them to engage with the parts of themselves that aren't burdened by the need to think. The need to think is, for the large part, that which does the most damage in a time where there are too many decisions to be made - and it's the reason that people who otherwise have issues with decision-making find it so hard to deal with open-ended decisions.

This emotional state feels exhilarating, and for certain kinds of people, it is addictive. It's not preposterous to ask why - needing to be redlined into an adaptive state isn't something that people might want is predictable in those who are primed to react to changing environments, whether or not a threat. It means that these sorts of people are hypervigilant, but it also means that there is a portion of the population that is particularly good in a time of crisis because this is when their brain works the best.

The uncommon, for these kinds of people, are a time to reset our minds.
