## Validator Tiers:
There are four types of validators:
- [[in-house validator]]
- [[institutional validator]]
- [[retail validator]]
- [[activist validator]]

### 1. In-House validators
An [[in-house validator]] is often a validator operation that has been subsumed by a parent organization, usually a [[cryptocurrency exchange]] that is seeking to support a token internally and hopes to provide a reward rate to their customers on this basis, as well as skimming off of this inflationary reward to prevent the dilution of their own holdings against the market.

In these situations, the holdings of an exchange are often delegated to a single or a small number of accounts and the accounts internal to the exchange are not represented on the network for which the exchange's validators provide their services.

The margins are, in this way, extremely low, since these validators do can dictate commission on the basis of total asset holdings delegated to their operating validators. The requirements for participating in [[protocol governance]] are as well low, since the customers that an [[in-house validator]] serve are the retail token holders who custody their assets within the [[cryptocurrency exchange]], thus there being little incentive to involve themselves in the development of the protocols they support themselves, since they are likely more focused on a cohesive product experience for their customers.

## 2. Institutional Validators
An [[institutional validator]] is often an operation whose profit margins depends on delegation by institutional-level holders of tokens in [[blockchain]] and [[cryptocurrency]] networks. The targets of this type of validator are individual wallet accounts, usually investors in protocols themselves, or foundations that are holding portions of the assets for a network.
As such, this kind of validator is primarily concerned with a [[small-but-mighty]] clientele that is interested in the success of the networks that they support, but as an infrastructure provider, do not feel any real obligation to the maintenance of the network from a [[protocol governance]] perspective, due to a lack of [[incentive]] to do so. Institutional validators can operate white-label validator nodes at 100% commission on-chain, then settle with the investor off-chain.
Institutional validators may operate on a large number of chains of varying complexity on the basis of the desires and demands of institutional-level tokenholders.

## 3. Research / Retail Validators
A [[retail validator]] seeks to make their margins off of the percentage of commission that they earn from their stakers. As such, much of their success is contingent on how a community of tokenholders who are not at the institutional level and the reputation that they hold as an operator within the community of operators.
The means through which they can do this is through garnering popularity, participation in [[protocol governance]], and [[core contribution]] to [[protocol research]] - since the reputation and margin for the operation of a [[retail validator]] increases with the amount of stake delegated to their node.

## 4. Activist Validators
An [[activist validator]] operates at the scale of, usually, a single or only a few chains. They operate with the full intention to act committed to the success of the chain that they are a part of, and their main market are the community of tokenholders who hold a middling but self-custodied number of assets. In this sense, activist validators play the volume game, hoping to have as many assets custodied to them by members of the community so as to earn the support that they merit.

Paradoxically, [[activist validator]]s are often the most value-aligned with the success of a particular [[blockchain]] - they are normally highly active in [[protocol governance]], especially in systems that employ [[token-based governance]] under [[delegated proof-of-stake]]. The counter-play of this kind of validator is that they are often the most aligned with the vision of the chain, but are not rewarded accordingly other than by the hopes that the community recognize their contributions to the protocol even while often proselytizing the most on the value of the technology that they are supporting.
