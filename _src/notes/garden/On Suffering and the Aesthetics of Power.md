I've been reading a lot of [[Sontag]] lately - primarily because it's a book that was mentioned by [[Maggie Nelson]] in her book 'On Freedom', where Nelson speaks to the differences that exist for women or men in the pursuit of what she selects as the best definition *of* '[[freedom]]' - [[Foucault]]'s "patient labor giving form to our impatience for liberty".
Sontag's [[On Women]] includes an essay on [[Leni Riefenstahl]]'s career. Sontag is practically derisive of all the work she produced, focusing heavily on the aesthetics of how it reflects a [[fascist]] ideology. She then repeats something I hear her say in an earlier essay in the collection, where she says, explicitly, that any improvements in the relations between the genders will require men ceding their [[power]] to women. Particularly, she quotes:
```Fascist art glorifies surrender, it exalts mindlessness, it glamorizes death.```
I'm fascinated with this description of specifically fascist aesthetics because it leads me in a different direction than that I think Sontag was implying. Much of the time, the sort of logic that I hear espoused by people who are addressing their frustration with the unknown and the burden of having to calculate one's own purpose with in the [[search for meaning]], people turn to [[Buddhism]], and particularly toward an interpretation that 'all life is suffering'. I don't end up seeing how different this is from what Sontag is saying here - because I think there is a sort of 'mindlessness' in taking an approach to the experience of being a person that comes from the [[nihilism|nihilist]] approach of believing that nothing matters and that there is somehow something 'blissful' about letting this go.
A book that I haven't read yet but that I have on my shelf right now is [[Schopenhauer]]'s "[[Studies in Pessimism]]". I bought it and have intended to read it because it describes a contrarian view to the body of work I have been pursuing in the last few months - writing and reading embattled mostly with a quiet pursuit of [[hope]]. The personal reasons I have for this are that I tried working on something hopeful and —surrounded by an industry and a culture primarily populated by men—ended up starting to feel as though it wasn’t worth actually working against the grain of a society built by and for men. The frank truth was that in the midst of all the ‘hard work’ I’d found myself doing over the last few years, I had begun to feel that I was in the pursuit of other people’s goals.
[[Maureen Murdock]]’s “The Heroine’s Journey” acts as a counterpoint to the well-known [[Joseph Campbell]] book of a comparative subject.
