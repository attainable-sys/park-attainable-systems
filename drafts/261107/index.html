Grid Economics

The Line Before the Line

Most new power plants never fail on engineering. They stall in a waiting room most people have never heard of.

Mockup text · 6 min read

Before a solar farm or a battery can sell a single kilowatt-hour, it has to ask permission to plug in. The request goes into an interconnection queue run by the regional grid operator, and the queue decides who studies the project, when, and what upgrades the developer will be asked to pay for.

For most of the twentieth century this was a quiet process. A utility planned a plant, studied its own wires, and built both. The queue only became a bottleneck once thousands of independent developers began proposing projects at once, many of them speculative, many in the same windy or sunny places where the existing lines were already full.

The bottleneck is not the panel or the turbine. It is the paperwork that decides who pays for the wire.

Each request triggers studies of how the new plant would change power flows across the network. When one project withdraws, the studies of everyone behind it can change, and the cycle restarts. Developers file extra requests to hedge, which lengthens the line for everyone.

Grid operators have started to batch requests into clusters, raise deposits, and require proof that a developer controls the land. These reforms trade speed for seriousness. Whether they shorten the wait or only change who waits is the open question.

What makes the queue hard to see is that it produces no ribbon cuttings. Its output is an absence: plants that were designed, financed on paper, and then quietly dropped when the upgrade bill arrived years later.
